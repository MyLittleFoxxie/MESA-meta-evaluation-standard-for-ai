Not applicable: the issue does not apply. For a question asking whether checking or testing occurred, Yes means the stated checks occurred for all parts of the question, not that results were favourable or every error was eliminated. Definitions:
AI: Software that can perform tasks such as answering questions or working with text and images. The software taking a test may also use extra tools.
AI benchmark: A test used to assess an AI system. It contains problems, a way to mark answers and rules for interpreting results.
Benchmark version: A particular edition of a test. Its problems, answers or marking rules may differ from another edition, so the edition matters when comparing scores.
Test problem: One question or puzzle that the AI must answer or solve.
Ability: Something the AI is meant to be able to do, such as finding a pattern or answering a science question. Doing well on one skill does not automatically show every skill.
Score: The result of marking answers, such as the percentage correct. A score has meaning only in relation to the test and the conditions used.
Scoring: The rules or software that turn an AI’s answers into points or a result.
Reference answer: The supplied answer against which the AI’s response is marked. It can be wrong, so agreement with it is not proof that it is correct.
Prompt: The directions and information given to the AI before it answers.
Score uncertainty: How much a score estimate could vary under the assumptions of a calculation. It differs from the AI saying it is confident about an answer.
Intended use: The comparison, claim or decision that a score is meant to support.
AI judge: Another AI that marks a response by following instructions, often by comparing it with a supplied answer. Its marking can also make mistakes.
Comparison point: A result used to help interpret a score, such as random guessing, a person’s score or another AI’s score.
Repeated testing: Running the same test more than once to see whether the scores change.
Prior exposure: The possibility that an AI encountered a test question or answer before taking the test, for example during its training.
Development: The process of creating, selecting and checking the test problems and scoring rules.
Training: The process in which an AI learns from data before it is used. If test answers are included, later test scores may reflect earlier exposure.
Evaluation set: The collection of problems used to assess an AI. It may be separate from problems available for practice.
Open-ended research: Work where the answer or next step is not fixed in advance, such as investigating a new scientific question.
General intelligence: A broad ability to handle many different kinds of problems. A high score on one test does not establish this by itself.
Completed HLE teaching example (not an answer key for other benchmarks):
Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE)

Imagine a very difficult exam assembled by specialists from many subjects. The AI must select an answer or give a short answer; some questions also include an image. A separate AI marks the response against a supplied answer. HLE aims to measure advanced academic knowledge and reasoning. You do not need to know the subject answers to evaluate how this test is built, marked and interpreted.

Which test: The original 2,500-question HLE described in the supplied paper, with the simple public evaluation code inspected on 7 October 2026. This survey does not cover HLE-Rolling, HLE-Diamond or their evaluations with extra tools.

Intended use: Using scores to compare AI systems on difficult academic questions, and considering claims about academic expertise beyond the exact questions.

What the AI is asked to do

Humanity’s Last Exam, or HLE, aims to assess advanced academic knowledge and reasoning in AI systems that handle text or text and images. This survey focuses on **the original HLE, which contains 2,500 difficult questions**, rather than the separate HLE-Rolling and HLE-Diamond versions. About **24% of its questions are multiple-choice**, while the rest require short answers, and about **14% include an image**. A published biology example asks for a number about the anatomy of a hummingbird bone. You do not need to solve that problem to complete this survey; it illustrates the kind of specialist question the AI receives. Across these formats, HLE asks for specific answers rather than the completion of an open-ended research project.

How the questions were chosen and checked

Experts from many institutions contributed questions covering a range of subjects, with particular emphasis on mathematics. Before expert review, questions that selected AI models could already answer were screened out, so the resulting set was **deliberately chosen to be difficult**, rather than randomly drawn from everyday academic work. HLE’s creators describe their development process in a research paper, which reports expert review and organizer approval, followed by two further checks of 200 questions each. After the second check, the authors estimated an **expert-disagreement rate of 15.4%**. That figure concerns disagreements about questions or answers; it should not be read as the percentage confirmed to be wrong. Because the test requires specific answers, it also leaves creative work and open-ended research outside its answer format.

Instructions and marking

HLE’s public evaluation software gives the AI each question, includes an image when needed, and asks it to return an explanation, a final answer and a percentage stating its confidence. The instructions for that software describe a default “temperature” of zero, a setting intended to reduce randomness, but the line that sends this setting is **disabled in the inspected code**. Once a response is produced, a separate **AI judge compares the final answer with the supplied reference answer**, accepting different wording when the meaning is the same. Because the judge is instructed to compare answers rather than solve the problem independently, agreement does not establish that the reference itself is correct. The judging code is public, but the inspected sources contain no full study comparing its marks with expert marks. The expert reviews of the questions described earlier therefore do not, by themselves, establish that the automatic marking is accurate.

What the reported numbers mean

HLE reports **accuracy as the percentage of answers marked correct**, with each question counting equally. As a result, the mix of subjects affects the total score. The paper also reports results by subject, although the inspected material does not establish that these are separate measures of distinct skills. Alongside accuracy, the public scoring software calculates an **approximate 95% confidence range** using the number of questions and the fraction marked correct. This range describes score uncertainty under the calculation’s assumptions; it does not measure the effects of repeating the AI run or changing the judge. A separate calculation compares the AI’s stated confidence with how often its answers are correct, so **confidence in an answer and uncertainty in a test score refer to different things**.

Comparisons and repeated testing

To put results in context, the paper compares named AI models and reports scores for both the full question set and text-only questions. Although experts helped create the questions, this packet contains **no matched group of people taking the whole exam under the AI testing conditions**. The paper also notes that an AI can answer differently on different attempts, but the inspected sources do not provide a repeated-run study covering the main comparisons. Nor do they provide a systematic study of reworded instructions or changes to the judging AI. Using a fixed setting or a standard instruction therefore does not, on its own, establish how stable the reported scores are.

Prior exposure and ways to earn points

To reduce easy ways of gaining points, contributors were asked to avoid questions answerable by a simple lookup and could revise questions when an AI guessed correctly through faulty reasoning. Multiple-choice questions still allow guessing, however, and the supplied material does not measure every possible way of earning points without the intended knowledge or reasoning. The authors also retain a **private test set** to help assess whether improvements are specific to the public questions. For the public data, a distinctive text marker helps people identify material to exclude from future AI training, while the instructions warn against training on the test. These precautions do not establish what a particular AI has already seen. The packet also lacks a complete original-HLE policy covering repeated submissions, access to feedback and the reporting of systems trained specifically on the test.

Access, fair comparisons and updates

Fair comparisons depend partly on which questions each system receives. Systems that use images and those that only use text do not necessarily answer the same set, so the paper labels text-only results. The questions are in English and subjects are not equally represented; the supplied material contains no comprehensive study of comparisons across languages or populations. Public instructions and marking code allow some inspection, but downloading the dataset requires accepting contact-sharing conditions, and the private set is not released. The dataset page lists an MIT licence and asks users not to redistribute test data, while the code also has an MIT licence. The authors describe excluding certain kinds of dangerous content during collection. The survey preparer inspected the available documentation without downloading the gated questions or rerunning the AI evaluations. When comparing results over time, the edition also matters: HLE-Rolling has its own record of removed and restored questions, and **those changes do not automatically describe original HLE**. No study establishing equivalent scores across original HLE, HLE-Rolling and HLE-Diamond is supplied here.

What the findings cover

HLE’s title suggests a final exam at the edge of human knowledge, but the paper limits its interpretation to difficult academic questions with definite answers. It explicitly states that **a high score alone does not establish general intelligence or the ability to conduct independent research**. Consistent with that limit, this packet contains no measured relationship between HLE scores and success on independent scientific projects. Such gaps leave broader claims unresolved; they do not, by themselves, show that the benchmark fails.

How an HLE answer is marked

Receive a question: Text, sometimes with an image; choose an option or give a short answer.

Give a response: The AI provides an explanation, a final answer and its stated confidence.

Compare with the reference: A separate AI judge checks the final answer against the supplied answer. Equivalent meanings can count.

Process diagram based on the simple evaluation code. The judge is not asked to solve the question independently.

Three different numbers in the results

Accuracy: What percentage of answers were marked correct?

Score range: How uncertain is that percentage under the calculation used? This is not a repeated-run test.

Confidence check: Does how confident the AI says it is match how often it is correct?

These are separate summaries of the answers. None substitutes for measuring changes across repeated runs.

Questions and matching HLE examples:
1. [Description] Are the benchmark version, set of test problems and method for calculating scores identified?
Meaning: Identify the edition of the benchmark, the problems used, and the scoring method. This asks whether all three are stated, not whether they are good choices.
HLE example: Yes. The passage identifies original HLE, its 2,500 questions and the inspected scoring software. This tells readers which test and scoring method the example concerns.

2. [Description] Are the ability being tested, the intended AI systems and the intended uses described?
Meaning: Look for the skill being tested, which AI systems the test is intended for, and the proposed uses of its scores. A description of these aims is different from evidence that the test achieves them.
HLE example: Yes. HLE describes difficult academic knowledge and reasoning, the AI systems it targets and the comparisons it is intended to support.

3. [Description] Are the test problems, the information provided and the required answers or actions described?
Meaning: The description should say what the AI receives and what it must produce or do. You do not need to solve the problems yourself.
HLE example: Yes. The passage describes short-answer and multiple-choice questions, images when needed, and the answers the AI must return.

4. [Description] Are the instructions, resources and limits for taking the test described?
Meaning: Testing conditions include instructions, tools, supplied documents, time, allowed attempts, and AI settings. This asks whether relevant conditions are described, not whether every possible condition was studied.
HLE example: Partly. The instructions and software are described, but the advertised setting to reduce randomness is disabled in the inspected code. A reader cannot assume that setting was used.

5. [Description] Is it described how answers or actions become scores and how results are presented?
Meaning: Look for the rules that turn responses into points, any averaging or weighting, and how results are shown. Describing a scoring rule does not establish that it is accurate or appropriate.
HLE example: Yes. The public code explains how a judging AI marks answers and how accuracy and confidence-related results are calculated. Describing these rules does not establish that they work well.

6. [Purpose and development] Is it explained why the test problems measure the ability the benchmark claims to test?
Meaning: The explanation should link the problems to the claimed skill. Being difficult or requiring specialist knowledge does not by itself establish that a task measures reasoning.
HLE example: Yes. The paper explains why hard, specialist questions with definite answers are used to assess academic knowledge and reasoning. Whether they cover the full claim is a separate question.

7. [Purpose and development] Are the origins and choice of test problems justified for the benchmark’s intended use?
Meaning: Look for reasons for the sources, number, topics, and difficulty of problems in relation to the intended use. Availability alone is not a reason that these problems represent the skill.
HLE example: Partly. Experts contributed questions and the authors explain the difficulty screening. However, selecting questions that AI systems struggle with does not show that the set represents academic work more generally.

8. [Purpose and development] Were the test problems and the answers used to mark them checked for errors during development?
Meaning: This asks whether both the problems and the supplied answers used to mark them were examined for mistakes during development. It is separate from testing how new AI responses are marked. Evidence that examination occurred does not establish that every answer is correct; note limits in its coverage when relevant.
HLE example: Yes. Expert review and later checks examined the questions and supplied answers. Remaining expert disagreement limits confidence in those answers, but does not undo the evidence that checking occurred.

9. [Purpose and development] Is there evidence that the test problems cover the relevant parts of the ability being claimed?
Meaning: A skill may have several relevant parts. Look for evidence that the selected problems cover the parts included in the claim. A narrow set of problems may fit a narrow claim but not a broader one.
HLE example: Partly. The questions cover many subjects, but emphasize mathematics and require definite answers. They therefore cover some academic skills while leaving creative work and open-ended research outside the format.

10. [Scoring and comparisons] Is there evidence that the way points are awarded reflects the ability the benchmark is meant to measure?
Meaning: Look for evidence that earning more points means doing better at the intended skill. A fixed scoring rule can work exactly as written while rewarding formatting or other features unrelated to that skill.
HLE example: Partly. The judging AI accepts answers with the same meaning as the reference. However, a wrong reference or an incorrect judging decision can still affect the mark, so understanding is not the only possible influence.

11. [Scoring and comparisons] Is there evidence supporting how scores are combined and what any separate scores are said to measure?
Meaning: Look for reasons for combining results, weighting groups, and naming any separate skills. Averaging scores is a calculation; it does not by itself establish that the total measures one ability.
HLE example: Partly. Each question counts equally, so the subject mix affects the overall score. The inspected material does not establish that the subject scores are separate measures of distinct skills.

12. [Scoring and comparisons] Was the marking process tested to see whether it awards the right scores consistently?
Meaning: Marking can be done by people, fixed software rules, or another AI. This asks whether that process was tested for correct and consistent marks. Testing that found mistakes still counts as testing. Fixed software rules alone do not show that tests occurred, and evidence of some testing does not establish complete marking accuracy.
HLE example: Not sure. The judging code is public, but no full comparison of its marks with expert marks is supplied. Checking the questions is different from checking whether the judging AI marks responses correctly.

13. [Scoring and comparisons] Are the comparison points used to explain the scores justified for the claims being made?
Meaning: A comparison point is a human score, another AI score, or a guessing result used to interpret a score. Ask whether it fits the stated claim and conditions. Comparing particular systems does not establish a ranking among all systems or a justified score cutoff for a decision.
HLE example: Partly. Named AI models provide useful comparison points, and text-only results are labelled. However, there is no matched group of people taking the whole test under the AI conditions.

14. [Consistency of results] Was it tested how much the same AI system’s score changes when the benchmark is run more than once?
Meaning: This asks whether the same AI was evaluated repeatedly under the same conditions and score changes were measured. One reported evaluation does not establish whether other evaluations occurred; an explicit statement that only one occurred rules out repetition for that evaluation. Neither establishes that repeated scores would be unstable.
HLE example: Not sure. The paper notes that answers can change between attempts, but the supplied sources do not establish a repeated-run study for the main comparisons. That leaves the amount of variation unclear.

15. [Consistency of results] Was it tested how much scores change when instructions, presentation or the testing setup change?
Meaning: This asks whether effects of changed instructions, presentation, or settings were measured. Repeating identical conditions is different from changing conditions. Findings about one change need not cover every possible change.
HLE example: Not sure. The supplied sources do not establish systematic tests of reworded instructions or changes to the judging AI. A standard instruction alone does not show what happens when the setup changes.

16. [Consistency of results] Are estimates of how much scores could vary provided in enough detail to support the comparisons or decisions being made?
Meaning: Look for a range or other estimate of possible score differences, how it was calculated, and whether it supports the intended comparison. An estimate based on the number of problems need not measure changes across repeated evaluations; small score differences may remain unresolved.
HLE example: Partly. The code calculates an approximate confidence range, but it does not cover changes from repeating the run or changing the judge. It therefore describes only some of the uncertainty relevant to comparisons.

17. [Meaning and fairness] Were ways of earning points without using the ability being tested investigated?
Meaning: A shortcut earns points without the intended skill, for example through accidental clues. This asks whether such routes were investigated. Identifying a possible shortcut does not establish that a tested AI used it; an investigation that found a problem still occurred.
HLE example: Partly. Contributors were asked to avoid easy lookups and could revise questions answered through faulty reasoning. These checks address some ways to gain points, but do not measure every possible route.

18. [Meaning and fairness] Was the risk that AI systems had already seen the test problems or answers assessed and addressed?
Meaning: Look for both an assessment of earlier exposure to questions or answers and steps to address the risk. Public availability may create a risk without proving that a particular model saw the material. Protective measures do not establish that exposure was impossible.
HLE example: Partly. A private set, a training-data marker and warnings help address prior exposure. They do not establish which questions a particular AI has already seen.

19. [Meaning and fairness] Are the rules on training specifically for this test and repeatedly submitting results justified for the intended use?
Meaning: Look for rules about training specifically for the test and repeatedly submitting results, with reasons linked to the intended use. A rule being absent from the supplied description is different from evidence that the rule is unsuitable.
HLE example: Not sure. Training on the test is discouraged, but the supplied material lacks a complete policy on repeated submissions and feedback. There is not enough information to judge whether those rules are suitable.

20. [Meaning and fairness] Is there evidence for any claim that the scores show how well an AI system will perform beyond the problems in this test?
Meaning: This concerns claims about new problems or real work beyond the benchmark. Look for evidence covering that broader claim. A stated ambition alone is not such evidence; if no broader claim or use arises, consider Not applicable.
HLE example: Not sure. The passage supplies no measured link between HLE scores and success on independent scientific projects. This leaves wider performance claims unresolved rather than proving they are false.

21. [Meaning and fairness] Is there evidence that comparisons are fair across the AI systems and situations the benchmark is meant to cover?
Meaning: Look for evidence that differences in information, tools, tasks, resources, or settings do not make the intended comparison misleading. Findings about differences between people do not by themselves establish fair comparisons between AI systems.
HLE example: Partly. Text-only results are distinguished from results using images, which helps readers compare like with like. Unequal subject coverage and limited checks across languages leave other comparisons less well supported.

22. [Use and reporting] Are the test materials and methods available in enough detail for someone else to repeat the evaluation or examine how it was done?
Meaning: Look for enough problems, scoring details, instructions, and software to repeat or inspect the evaluation. Public access to some files does not necessarily reproduce hidden test results or earlier software versions.
HLE example: Partly. Instructions and marking code are public, but the dataset is gated and the private set is unavailable. These materials support inspection without providing everything needed to examine all results.

23. [Use and reporting] Are the rules for accessing and reusing the benchmark, and the protections needed for responsible use, stated?
Meaning: Look for access and reuse rules and relevant safeguards, such as protecting hidden answers. A missing permission statement does not establish that reuse is prohibited. This asks whether relevant rules are stated, not whether all materials must be public.
HLE example: Partly. Licences, access conditions and harmful-content precautions are described. The combination of a licence and a request not to redistribute the data leaves some reuse expectations less clear to readers.

24. [Use and reporting] Are changes to the benchmark recorded, including how they affect comparisons between old and new scores?
Meaning: Look for a record of changes to problems, answers, or scoring and an explanation of their effects on old and new scores. A new version number alone does not explain whether scores can be compared.
HLE example: Not sure. Changes to HLE-Rolling are described, but that is a separate test. The supplied material does not establish how changes to original HLE affect comparisons of old and new scores.

25. [Use and reporting] Do the published claims stay within what the evidence supports?
Meaning: Compare what is publicly claimed with the tasks, conditions, and results actually reported. A claim about these tasks may be justified even when a broader claim is not. Missing evidence for a broad claim does not prove that the AI lacks the ability.
HLE example: Partly. The paper explicitly limits claims about general intelligence and independent research. However, the broad title can suggest more than the definite-answer academic questions establish.
