## MESA and ordinary review

Both selected MESA and ordinary reviews distinguish input-level exact grid success from fractional success across multiple inputs. MESA states: “A fractional problem score represents the proportion of new inputs solved, not a partly correct output grid.” Ordinary review similarly limits interpretation to the “average within-problem fraction of exactly solved inputs.” Both retain the passage's bounded grid interpretation rather than treating it as general intelligence.

The selected MESA ARC review says: “Matching test sets for human difficulty does not establish stable AI scores.” The ordinary review likewise identifies missing repeat-run estimates. Their DesignQA reviews distinguish changed passage sets from identical-condition repeatability. These are retained substantive distinctions, irrespective of category selection agreement.

MESA identifies documented DesignQA formatting/reference problems and calls the always-yes route an “inferred opportunity, not evidence that any tested AI used it.” The ordinary review makes the same distinction. Both explain that unavailable licensing information does not establish a prohibition on reuse. MESA and ordinary review therefore preserve several concerns and cautions in the same supplied text; this does not show that questionnaire structure caused those findings.

## Categories do not guarantee a justified negative evaluation

In the MESA ARC review, documented_problems includes: “Exact matching gives no credit for a partly correct grid.” This repeats a scoring rule accurately, but the review does not explain why that rule is a defect for the stated exact-grid use. The analyst therefore flags the placement as an unsupported evaluative escalation, not as a false factual statement. The other listed scoring-description discrepancy is separately grounded in the passage. This observation must not be generalized to all MESA runs.

The ordinary ARC review places “Verified evaluation uses one run, leaving reported results vulnerable to unquantified run variation” under documented_problems. A one-run policy is documented, but actual harmful variation is not established by the passage. The wording points to a risk rather than demonstrating instability. This is a boundary needing interpretation, not proof that the benchmark is unstable or that all ordinary reviews mishandle missing information.

## Reported interpretation difficulties

MESA's ARC closing note says: “Items 8 and 12 can blur whether checking occurred with whether it was comprehensive.” It adds that one-run verified testing does not establish absence of other repeated-run research. Its DesignQA note says: “Items 12 and 17 leave some ambiguity about how much checking is enough.” These are generated reports of difficulty, not observed human comprehension or explanations of hidden model processes. The explicit instruction already distinguishes whether testing happened from favorable outcomes; the comments motivate scrutiny of adequacy thresholds rather than a claim that those instructions were absent.

## EFPA comparison: all fields inspected

The selected source is `real-efpa-neutral-1/response.txt`, for ARC and DesignQA. Its short `instrument_review` fields introduce the review, but detailed original-EFPA judgments appear in the common conclusion arrays. All those arrays and both closing notes were inspected. Their placement means neither summary length nor summary-only item counts describes the substantive review.

The EFPA ARC output retains each of the six passage concerns. Under scoring it says: “Input successes are averaged within problems, then problem scores are averaged.” It separately notes that the dataset's fully-solved terminology requires every input correct. Under precision it states: “Human difficulty matching cannot establish AI parallelism.” Its exposure discussion says safeguards reduce risk “without establishing its absence.” Public inspectability, hidden-data conditions and unrecalculated corrections appear separately. Finally, its fairness mapping states: “Human demographic findings do not establish AI fairness.” The last explicit distinction is retained in the ordinary review and compressed out of the selected questionnaire output, although the questionnaire output still limits comparisons by evaluation route and human-study scope.

The EFPA DesignQA output also retains the six planned concerns. It separates one-rulebook task relevance from cross-industry representativeness, observed punctuation/reference problems from missing validation, and unequal task counts from equal weighting. It preserves the “6.25 percentage points” consequence of one answer in the 16-question group, which appears in the ordinary review but is absent from the selected questionnaire output. Its shortcut statement adds: “No evidence shows that a tested AI used this strategy.” Its reuse statement adds: “This does not establish prohibited reuse or absence of permission elsewhere.” It distinguishes varied passages from repeated identical conditions and warns that input variants do not establish equivalent forms. These are passage-grounded distinctions, not evidence that one method finds them more often.

### Substantive retention and compression

The original-EFPA application separates several technical questions that the questionnaire output compresses. For ARC it treats identical-test stability, alternate-form equivalence, agreement during reference development, and evidence for intended response processes separately. For DesignQA it explicitly says: “Two additional reviewers are not evidence of adequate agreement by themselves.” The questionnaire's item 8 records limited checking and its item 12 records scoring tests, but its selected output does not explicitly request the reviewers' agreement coefficient; the ordinary output retains the disagreement-rate gap. The distinction is between checks taking place and agreement evidence supporting those checks, rather than a claim that additional reviewers supplied no useful evidence.

EFPA also distinguishes descriptive task results, normative reference interpretations and competence decisions. Its ARC statement, “The 407 people and 120-problem sets are not AI norm samples,” prevents confusing human or item counts with a population of assessed AI systems. For DesignQA it states: “Engineering compliance decisions and item answer keys are not competency cut scores.” Neither selected questionnaire nor ordinary output explicitly makes those norm-sample or competence-threshold distinctions, because their conclusions do not propose those interpretations. Their absence is therefore a loss of technical explicitness rather than a demonstrated failure for the stated task-comparison use.

The questionnaire output, in turn, makes AI-specific lifecycle issues easier to locate. ARC item 19 says: “Development training is allowed, evaluation-specific adaptation is discouraged, and verified feedback is not offered for repeated improvement.” DesignQA item 19 identifies unavailable training and repeated-submission rules. The EFPA output retains these concerns under 6.3.7 and its closing material, rather than losing them; it also retains exposure, scoring-software and historical-configuration concerns. No examined passage concern is established as uniquely recoverable through the questionnaire in this selected sample. Original-EFPA technical distinctions and questionnaire-specific prompts organize overlapping evidence differently.

### Unsupported classification and coverage cautions

The EFPA DesignQA output places “6.3.7 = 1 — Clear rules for training on released questions and repeated submissions were unavailable in the examined sources” in `documented_problems`. Its own rating key defines 0 as insufficient information and 1 as inadequate. The quoted evidence establishes an inspection gap; it does not establish that the underlying rules are inadequate. The classification therefore needs qualification or a clearer rationale. This parallels the need to distinguish facts, risks and demonstrated defects in the other two selected methods.

Some EFPA judgments depend on choices not settled by the source passage. For example, its use of n/a for internal consistency is justified by heterogeneous task aggregation; that is an explicit reviewer position about applicability, not an empirical finding proving the score's structure. Its normative and three-report requirements introduce information demands beyond the questionnaire. The output itself limits the norm judgment: “this does not make raw grid-performance scores defective.” Those conditions should remain attached to any reported deficiency or restrictive final recommendation.

Both reviews claim that no numbered field or rated item was intentionally omitted, and refer to a 7.2.8/7.2.7 numbering inconsistency. This audit does not certify those claims or check the source PDF. It establishes substantive coverage of the twelve preselected passage concerns and inspects illustrative human-to-AI mappings across all response fields. Some entries group multiple numbers under one rationale and many mark information absent or a question inapplicable. Neither that grouping, the number of references, nor the output's length proves complete or correct application of all EFPA requirements. No individual EFPA rating or final research-use recommendation is treated as an answer key.

## Limits

This selected output sample can illustrate how a review preserves a concern or makes an unsupported classification. It cannot establish omission rates or comparative accuracy. Simple controlled-probe classifications and real review prose are different tasks; success on the former does not certify the latter. Any broader frequency claim requires an explicitly defined audit of the full relevant set, not extrapolation from these examples.
