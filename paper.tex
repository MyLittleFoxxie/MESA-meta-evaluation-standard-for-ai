\begin{abstract}
AI benchmark scores are used to support capability claims, but the tests behind those scores also need review. MESA adapts the European Federation of Psychologists' Associations (EFPA) Test Review Model to AI benchmarks, drawing on BetterBench, construct-validity research, and literature on broad cognitive abilities. Part A records benchmark facts; Part B asks what interpretations the available evidence supports. The contribution is the adaptation and an initial examination of how it works. We revisit existing AI-assisted reviews of Humanity's Last Exam, ARC-AGI-2, and DesignQA to identify unclear ratings and limits of the review process. The two draft reviewers differed on seven of 21 section ratings. Their reasons reveal a need to distinguish published run settings from measured stability and to state the use being judged. Revised guidance was examined through four fictional examples and focused readings of HumanEval and TruthfulQA materials. These checks illustrate selected questions and expose a scorer-check omission in the short form. They are not independent validation. Human review time, agreement between reviewers, and effectiveness compared with other methods remain untested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

    Benchmarks are one of the main mechanisms by which AI systems are compared, ranked, marketed, and interpreted. In practice, benchmark scores are often treated as direct indicators of capability and reused as evidence for model selection, procurement, safety claims, and public narratives about what AI systems can do \cite{singh_leaderboard_illusion,alonso_church_sigir}. However, a benchmark score is not a capability itself. It is an inference drawn from a designed measurement instrument that operates under assumptions about content, administration, scoring, comparison, and interpretation. Benchmark quality is therefore central to whether AI model capability claims should be trusted.
    
    Recent research has identified multiple weaknesses in AI benchmarking practice, including benchmark contamination (i.e., test items leaking into model training data), prompt sensitivity (scores shift significantly when prompt wording changes), saturation (i.e., top models scoring near the ceiling, leaving insufficient room to discriminate between them), over-interpretation of leaderboard rankings beyond what the underlying numbers support, unclear definitions of what the benchmark is supposed to measure, weak documentation, and poor reproducibility \cite{jo_wilson_what_does_your_benchmark_measure}. These problems threaten construct validity --- whether the benchmark actually measures the capability it claims to measure: a model may score well because the benchmark is narrow, fragile, leaky, or poorly governed rather than because the model is genuinely capable.
    
    This paper argues that AI benchmarks should be reviewed with the same rigor as measurement instruments used to evaluate human cognitive ability. In human assessment, mature disciplines do not only score people on tests; they also review the tests themselves. The EFPA \textit{Test Review Model}, informed by the \textit{Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing}, offers a structured template for examining whether a test's content, scoring, evidence base, and interpretive claims are justified \cite{aera_apa_ncme_standards_2014,efpa_test_review_model_2025}. AI benchmarking lacks a similarly established review practice, despite the fact that benchmark results increasingly shape research agendas, procurement decisions, public claims about AI capabilities, and policy debates.
    
    The core operational requirement for benchmark use is \textit{traceability}: an unbroken documented path from the claimed capability, through task design, metric, and score, to the comparison basis and intended use, so that every step of a score interpretation can be checked against its evidence. A score is only as trustworthy as the measurement system that produced it, and that system's tasks, prompts, access conditions, scoring rules, baselines, and reporting surfaces may be poorly specified, unstable, or misaligned with the capability claim they support \cite{singh_leaderboard_illusion,line_goes_up}, propagating undetected measurement weaknesses into safety, fairness, and reliability decisions. This is already observable in software and requirements-engineering practice, where benchmarks that increasingly define agentic-system requirements --- scoring whether an agent can resolve repository-level issues, operate a terminal, or solve competitive programming tasks --- become the verification-and-validation evidence cited to accept or reject a requirement, yet rarely undergo the same scrutiny themselves.
    
    To address this gap, this paper proposes \textbf{MESA} (\textit{Meta-Evaluation Standard for AI Benchmarks}), a framework for reviewing what benchmark scores support. The contribution is an adaptation of EFPA and an initial examination of its use, rather than an established certification standard. MESA combines EFPA's description and evaluation structure, BetterBench's documentation and engineering concerns \cite{reuel_betterbench}, and research on the relationship between tasks, metrics, and capability claims \cite{bean_measuring_what_matters,hendrycks_definition_agi}. It can identify missing support; it cannot guarantee that a benchmark is robust or free from contamination.
    
    With this, the paper aims to address three research questions:
    \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
        \item \textbf{RQ1}: How should EFPA's questions be adapted to AI benchmarks, and why?
        \item \textbf{RQ2}: What works, what is unclear, and what takes too much effort when applying MESA?
        \item \textbf{RQ3}: Does the revised MESA work as expected on new benchmarks and small examples designed to test specific questions?
    \end{enumerate}
    
    MESA is intended to help researchers and requirements engineers state which uses of a benchmark score are supported, what information is missing, and where further study is needed.

\section{Related Work}

    \subsection{AI Benchmarking Practices and Challenges}
    
        Concerns across the AI benchmarking literature cluster around two questions: what a benchmark actually measures and how its scores are used, such that a recurring concern is the validity and scope of evaluations \cite{alonso_church_sigir}. Critiques find that popular benchmarks are often narrow, fragile, or unclear about their target construct, and that headline numbers can reflect saturation or shortcut exploitation rather than the capability they are taken to indicate \cite{jo_wilson_what_does_your_benchmark_measure,line_goes_up}. Regarding how scores are used, work on leaderboard practices documents systematic over-interpretation and distortion --- selective comparison, unreported evaluation conditions, and ranking instability --- that make leaderboard positions weaker evidence than their public presentation suggests \cite{singh_leaderboard_illusion}. Contamination and selective reporting worsen the problem: as benchmarks age and leak into training corpora, memorization inflates scores while competitive pressure incentivizes gaming and cherry-picking \cite{cheng_benchmarking_broken}. A related failure is benchmark drift, where an instrument is repurposed beyond its original design --- as when the Penn Treebank \cite{marcus_etal_1993_building}, built for syntactic parsing, was later reused as a perplexity benchmark in ways that detached the metric from its intended meaning \cite{alonso_church_sigir}.
        
        In response, the community has emphasized documentation and standardization. Datasheets for datasets and model cards establish structured reporting of provenance, intended use, and limitations \cite{gebru_datasheets_2021}, while others score benchmarks against best-practice criteria spanning design, implementation, documentation, and maintenance \cite{reuel_betterbench}. These efforts are valuable but largely \emph{developer-facing} and artifact-level: they help creators build and document better benchmarks rather than help a downstream reviewer decide what an existing benchmark's scores are permitted to mean. MESA builds on and expands this literature by unifying these threads into a single, \emph{reviewer-facing} instrument-review template that separates factual description from evaluative judgment and disciplines the inferential chain from phenomenon to task to metric to claim. The gap MESA addresses is that AI benchmarks are widely used as measurement instruments, yet are rarely reviewed with a unified structure that ties every judgment to evidence, missing evidence, and a bounded statement of supported and non-supported use.
    
    \subsection{Benchmarking standards across the fields}
    
        Beyond the AI benchmarking literature, MESA is informed by review and measurement practices from adjacent fields --- requirements engineering, psychometric test review, construct-validity theory, and cognitive-ability research --- and we identify common themes regarding what makes a measurement instrument trustworthy. Where applicable, we adapt these practices into the MESA Test Review Model.
        
        \textbf{Requirements engineering for AI systems.} RE research emphasizes that AI development changes the subjects and practices of requirements work. Habiba et al. \cite{habiba_re4ai_maturity_2024} provide a systematic mapping of 126 primary studies, showing growth around elicitation and analysis but persistent challenges in specification, validation, and quality requirements. This motivates MESA's framing of benchmark claims, tasks, scoring rules, and reproducibility materials as requirements-relevant review objects rather than neutral background infrastructure.
        
        \textbf{Psychometric instrument review.} The EFPA Test Review Model \cite{efpa_test_review_model_2025} evaluates psychological and educational instruments in two parts: a descriptive part (identity, users, administration, scoring, reports) and an evaluative part (rationale, materials, norms, reliability, validity, fair use, and final interpretation), and cautions against mechanically averaging ratings where expert judgment is required. MESA adapts this structure because a benchmark score, like a test score, is not self-justifying: it requires evidence that the instrument supports the meaning users attach to it.
        
        \textbf{Construct validity and the phenomenon--task--metric--claim chain.} Bean et al. \cite{bean_measuring_what_matters} supply a psychometric validity lens, framing benchmark validity as a chain from the real-world phenomenon, through the chosen task and scoring metric, to the claim made about the score. This chain maps naturally onto requirements traceability, and MESA uses it to test whether a score is valid evidence for the requirement-like claim attached to it.
        
        \textbf{Broad-intelligence and AGI claims.} Hendrycks et al. \cite{hendrycks_definition_agi} confront the strongest benchmark claims --- those equating performance with Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) --- grounding AGI in Cattell--Horn--Carroll (CHC) theory as a breadth-weighted profile across cognitive domains, and reporting a 'jagged' profile in current models that overstates general competence. MESA draws the lesson of claim proportionality: strong performance on one task family is narrow task-level evidence unless a validity argument spans the relevant abilities. MESA does not require every benchmark to be a full cognitive battery and acknowledges that CHC interpretation remains debated \cite{canivez_chc_critique}.

\section{Methodology}

    This study develops a review framework and uses applications to identify improvements. The evaluation has three parts: a present-day check of the adaptation, analysis of the existing pilot drafts, and focused checks of revised instructions. This follows the distinction between improving a research artifact and independently testing its performance \cite{venable_feds}. The intended interpretations and uses determine which validation evidence is needed \cite{kane_validation}. The work does not establish usability for unfamiliar reviewers, agreement between human reviewers, or superiority over other review methods.

    \subsection{Framework Construction}
    
        The framework uses four adaptation operations: preserve a review function that transfers to AI; rename a human-testing term; replace a human-specific assumption; or add a question needed for AI evaluation. Human test takers become evaluated AI systems; population norms become relevant reference points, including model cohorts, chance and human baselines; digitally generated individual reports become papers, dashboards and leaderboards. Benchmark-specific additions concern prompts, scorer code, model-based grading, contamination and changing execution conditions. The repository records 15 benchmarks as construction inputs. That inventory identifies the design context, but does not by itself document which observation produced each item. The new question map in the replication package explains the current design; it is not a recovered record of every original author decision.
    
    \subsection{Adaptation and Evidence Integration}

        BetterBench informs implementation, documentation, maintenance, reproducibility and contamination questions \cite{reuel_betterbench}. Measuring what Matters informs the link between the claimed ability, task and metric \cite{bean_measuring_what_matters}. AGI and CHC literature informs broad-intelligence claims, without requiring a narrow benchmark to cover every cognitive domain \cite{hendrycks_definition_agi}. The question map covers 44 active numbered descriptive units and 87 rated items, with related sources, reasons and applicability notes. These are proposed correspondences, not proof that the adapted questions are complete. For example, EFPA's internal-consistency questions are not interchangeable with score uncertainty, and the adequacy of MESA's treatment of item structure remains open.
    
    \subsection{Evaluation Procedure and Limits}
    
        First, an AI-assisted reading compared the existing draft and final reviews, preserving the original ratings. Second, the instructions were revised to clarify the intended use, reuse source notes, and distinguish missing studies from observed problems. Third, the revised template was identified by a file hash and kept unchanged during four fictional examples and focused readings of two additional benchmarks. Instructions and expected behavior were written before results were recorded, but after an initial look at the real-case sources. The same AI assistant helped revise the framework and performed the checks. These are partial applications, not full new benchmark reviews, blind tests, or human observations. The records in \texttt{thesis/} disclose these limits and preserve source links. Independent reviewers, measured human effort and full new applications remain further work.
    
\section{The MESA Test Review Model}
    
    MESA reviews a specified benchmark version and a stated use of its scores. Part A records the facts; Part B judges whether the available evidence supports that use. Table~\ref{tab:mesa-review-structure} gives the structure. \emph{Answering RQ1}, the adaptation preserves EFPA's review functions while changing the subjects, reference points and technical checks. The source map makes those choices inspectable; their completeness and practical value still need testing.

    \paragraph{Part A, Description}
    Part A describes the benchmark before evaluating it. It records the benchmark's factual identity, classification, scoring procedure, outputs, and access or lifecycle materials, corresponding to Sections~1--5 of Table~\ref{tab:mesa-review-structure}. Part A does not assume that the benchmark is fully documented. If a benchmark provides only a paper, leaderboard, dataset, or partial repository, MESA records unavailable descriptive information as an explicit evidence gap. This prevents missing documentation from being silently filled in, and it gives Part B a clear factual basis for distinguishing documented weaknesses from unknowns.
    
    \paragraph{Part B, Evaluation}
    Part B evaluates whether the benchmark supports its intended score interpretation. It preserves EFPA's evaluative structure, represented in Sections~6--12 of Table~\ref{tab:mesa-review-structure}, but adapts those categories to AI benchmark concerns. In particular, MESA foregrounds reliability across runs, prompts, scorers, judges, model versions, and evaluation environments (Section~9). It also expands validity review to include contamination, gameability --- whether a system can obtain a high score through shortcuts, exposure, formatting, tuning, or benchmark-specific scaffolding rather than the target capability --- and claim proportionality: whether public claims stay within what the measured evidence supports (Section~10).
    
    MESA uses EFPA-style ratings of \texttt{n/a} and \texttt{0}--\texttt{4}. The rating \texttt{n/a} means that an attribute is not applicable to the reviewed benchmark. A \texttt{0} means that the attribute is applicable, but the available information is absent or insufficient for evaluation. A \texttt{1} means that evidence exists, but is inadequate for the benchmark's stated purpose. Ratings of \texttt{2}, \texttt{3}, and \texttt{4} mean the evidence is adequate, good, or excellent, respectively, for that purpose. This distinction is important: missing documentation is an evidence gap, not automatically proof of poor benchmark quality. The final evaluation therefore does more than report ratings. It states what the score can support, what it cannot support, and what conditions must be held fixed for comparisons to be meaningful, such as benchmark version, split, prompt, harness, scorer, comparator cohort, uncertainty, and run conditions. Like EFPA, MESA avoids mechanical averaging; it adds a traceable account of appropriate score use.
    
    \paragraph{Template coherence}
    The same source may answer different questions: a prompt can matter for usability, stability and interpretation. Reviewers should record that source once and explain the distinct judgment at each relevant item. Earlier repository feedback identified overlaps and led to four retired item IDs. The current revision retains the 87 rated items and clarifies their use; it does not claim that all remaining repetition has been removed or that 87 is an optimal count.
    
    \paragraph{MESA as its own measurement object}
    MESA itself needs evaluation. Its purpose is to help a reviewer judge whether a particular use of benchmark scores is supported. The reviewer reads sources, records facts and makes contextual judgments. The outputs are ratings, reasons, missing information and use limits. The adequacy of those outputs must be examined; neither the existence of a filled template nor agreement after editing proves that MESA works. The framework has no aggregate benchmark-quality score.
    
    \paragraph{Scope of MESA judgments}
    Two boundary conditions follow from this framing. First, MESA reviews the benchmark as a measurement instrument, not the model being evaluated. The meaning of a benchmark score still depends on model-side evidence. For example, the same high score has different implications if the model may have seen the test set during training, used benchmark-specific scaffolding, or was evaluated under a different prompt or retrieval setup. A complete capability argument must therefore combine a MESA review of the benchmark with model-side evidence, such as training data, prior capabilities, runtime scaffolding, and documentation artifacts like Model Cards \cite{mitchell_model_cards_2019}, as well as the intended use of the score. MESA supplies a necessary part of that argument, not the whole argument.
    
    Second, the multi-LLM process used in the pilot cases assists drafting and comparison. It does not replace expert judgment. Human review would be needed before relying on such outputs for consequential decisions. The present study does not show that MESA accelerates review or is ready for certification.

\section{Case Study Design}
\label{sec:demonstration}

    MESA was applied to three benchmarks selected to vary in task format, scoring procedure, and public framing: Humanity's Last Exam (HLE), ARC-AGI-2, and DesignQA. HLE is an expert-level academic question-answering benchmark with multiple-choice and short-answer items, including a partially multimodal subset; it uses a model-as-judge procedure to assess answer equivalence \cite{phan_etal_hle_2025}. ARC-AGI-2 consists of visual grid-transformation tasks with public tasks and hidden evaluation splits; it is scored by exact cell-level grid matching and is publicly framed around progress toward general intelligence \cite{chollet_arc_agi_2_2025}. DesignQA evaluates question answering over an engineering rule document and CAD-derived data; it uses lexical and semantic similarity metrics, including F1, BLEU-2, ROUGE-L, and Sentence-BERT, and is framed more narrowly as an engineering-documentation benchmark rather than an AGI benchmark \cite{doris_designqa_2024}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/mesa-test-case-pipeline.png}
    \caption{Case study review pipeline. Each benchmark's information sources (HLE, ARC-AGI-2, or DesignQA) were paired with the MESA Test Review Model and sent to two independent reviewer models. A supervising editor model reconciled the two resulting MESA drafts, resolving each discrepancy against the sources, to produce the final MESA evaluations.}
    \label{fig:mesa-test-case-pipeline}

    The pilot records describe two reviewer models (ChatGPT~5.5 and Claude Opus~4.7) drafting reviews without seeing each other's output, followed by reconciliation by Gemini~3.1~Pro. The resulting files contain factual descriptions, ratings and reasons. Using more than one model was intended to expose disagreements; no reduction in bias was measured. All three cases also appear in the construction inventory, so they are development cases, not independent tests of coverage. Their original files are retained unchanged.
    
    \paragraph{Worked example} For HLE Section 9, the first draft assigned 2 and described reliability as adequate for broad directional conclusions. The final review assigned 1 and emphasized fine-grained ranking. This suggests that the judgments may concern different uses, though the drafts do not establish the cause of the disagreement. The revised instructions require reviewers to state the use beside disputed ratings. They also distinguish a missing stability study from a study showing unacceptable instability. The historical ratings have not been recomputed under this new guidance.

    \subsection{Replication Package}
        
        The replication package is at \url{https://github.com/MyLittleFoxxie/MESA-meta-evaluation-standard-for-ai}. It contains the manuscript, source literature, template, generated scorecard and nine original pilot reviews. The \texttt{thesis/} folder adds the scope statement, version records, question map, pilot comparison, short guide, test instructions and limited test results. File hashes identify the framework version used for the new checks. The current checkout has no generated dataset export; it should not be described as a supplied artifact.

\section{Case Study Results}

    \subsection{Humanity's Last Exam (HLE)}
    
        MESA reviewed the public HLE materials \cite{phan_etal_hle_2025}: 2,500 public questions, a documented private held-out split, the scoring and evaluation code, and the public leaderboards. HLE covers multiple-choice and short-answer formats with partial image-conditioned items, automated grading that uses a separate AI model to decide whether an answer matches the reference (a model-as-judge approach), and metrics including accuracy, category-level performance, and calibration error\footnote{Calibration error measures the gap between a model's stated confidence and its accuracy.}. Because the grader is itself an AI model, MESA treats checking that grader against human judgments as core evidence \cite{cheng_benchmarking_broken}. Item development was expert-led, the dataset and scoring materials are open, and confidence logging exposes miscalibration.

        MESA also finds gaps in reliability, validation, and reporting. The grading model (\texttt{o3-mini}) has not been checked against human judges; the paper reports that experts disagree with each other on 15--18\% of items, and an independent chemistry and biology reanalysis estimates errors up to 30\% in some text-only subjects \cite{skarlinski_futurehouse_hle_2025}, which caps how high a score can meaningfully go. The public leaderboard\footnote{Public leaderboard available at \url{https://agi.safe.ai}, accessed 2026-05-18.} drops the paper's non-AGI caveats and ranks models by very small score differences, with no confidence interval\footnote{A confidence interval is a range around a reported score that shows how much it could vary by chance; without a suitable comparison analysis, the review cannot establish whether a small difference is meaningful.} to show whether those differences are real. Finally, the inspected materials do not include data on how much scores change when the same model is run again, or a human baseline measured under the benchmark's own constraints (no internet or external tools), so the review does not establish expert-equivalent model performance under matched conditions.
        
        MESA therefore supports using HLE to measure performance on closed-ended academic tasks (including some multimodal inputs) under the official prompting setup, and to diagnose overconfidence and miscalibration. The review does not support using HLE alone to justify fine-grained leaderboard rankings or 'expert-equivalent' claims, nor as evidence of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), autonomous research, open-ended scientific creativity, general cognitive versatility, or long-horizon planning\footnote{Long-horizon planning refers to multi-step tasks where an agent plans and executes actions across many consecutive turns.}.
    
    \subsection{ARC-AGI-2}
    
        MESA reviewed the public ARC-AGI-2 materials \cite{chollet_arc_agi_2_2025}: public training and evaluation task files, documented semi-private and private evaluation splits, and public leaderboards. Systems infer a generative rule from a few demonstration input-output pairs and must produce exact output grids for unseen test inputs. The task design uses a fixed format with scoring that counts an answer correct only when every cell of the grid matches, and open public tasks. It includes a human reference point from a 407-participant human difficulty-norming campaign (515 sessions, 13{,}405 attempts) requiring at least two independent human solves per task, contamination controls via secure semi-private and private splits backed by an offline Kaggle sandbox, and cost-per-task reporting.

        MESA also finds gaps in construct coverage, reliability, and comparability. The materials frame the benchmark as measuring AGI, psychometric intelligence, and fluid intelligence\footnote{Fluid intelligence refers to the capacity to solve novel problems without relying on prior knowledge or language skills.}, yet the task family omits language, world knowledge, memory, planning, social cognition, embodiment, tool use, long-horizon agency, auditory processing, and real-world transfer. With 120 tasks in the public and semi-private splits, a 1\% difference is about one task, yet headline scores carry no confidence interval and no measure of how much scores change when a model is run again, so the reviewed reporting does not establish whether small ranking differences are meaningful. The leaderboard also mixes unlabeled runtime environments---the Kaggle offline sandbox (12-hour budget on $4\times$ NVIDIA L4 GPUs) and hosted APIs with cost-bounded scaffolding\footnote{Scaffolding refers to the orchestration code, agentic loops, retrieval systems, and tool-access layers wrapped around the model.}---which limits cross-system comparison. Finally, the inspected materials do not include per-task category labels for the four core challenge types, and a documented 2-versus-3 trial inconsistency with the official pass@2 rule\footnote{Under pass@2, a task is solved if the model produces the correct grid in at most two attempts.} remains unresolved, so it cannot be checked whether the sub-categories form a coherent construct.
        
        MESA therefore supports using ARC-AGI-2 to measure exact-grid abstraction and rule-induction under specified split, attempt, verification, and compute-cost conditions, with comparisons requiring matched conditions or a justified account of their differences. The review does not support standalone claims of AGI preparedness, human-level versatility, or broad psychometric intelligence; it is AGI-relevant only as one narrow component within a larger evaluation portfolio.
        
    \subsection{DesignQA}

        MESA reviewed the public DesignQA materials \cite{doris_designqa_2024}: the dataset, the GitHub repository, and a manually updated leaderboard. Multimodal models answer 1,451 question-answer pairs across six subsets (Retrieval, Compilation, Definition, Presence, Dimension, and Functional Performance) drawn from the Formula SAE (FSAE) 2024 rule document and MIT Motorsports CAD data, under a full-context (AllRules) or retrieval-augmented (RAG) condition. It is the most requirements-engineering-focused of the three cases. It uses real source materials, expert authoring with non-author review, an error analysis, and a paper that states its limits and makes no AGI claims.

        MESA also finds gaps in reliability, validation, and maintenance. DesignQA reports single numbers with no range showing uncertainty and no repeated runs for the main model comparisons. This matters most for the smaller subsets: Functional Performance contains only 16 items, so one item changes accuracy by 6.25 percentage points. The benchmark is also fully public, and the paper discusses fine-tuning on the Retrieval questions, which would amount to training on released test items. Scoring depends on the exact format and wording of the answer: it requires a strict format, compares against a single reference answer, and uses automatic word-overlap scores (BLEU and ROUGE)\footnote{BLEU and ROUGE are automatic scores that measure how much a model's answer overlaps, word for word, with a reference answer; they can mark a correct answer wrong when it is worded differently or has a different length.}, so a correct paraphrase may receive a lower similarity score. The size of any resulting error requires a separate scorer study. Finally, the inspected materials do not include a human baseline, a license file stating how the data may be reused (so reuse rights are unclear), or versioned releases.

        MESA therefore supports using DesignQA as a diagnostic benchmark for multimodal models on Formula SAE requirements QA, especially when results are reported by subset and the context condition is held fixed. The review does not establish a universal percentage-point threshold for meaningful differences; that requires an analysis suited to the compared results. The review does not support fine-grained model ranking, direct comparison by the simple-average score, procurement use, or broad claims about engineering competence. It also cautions against directly comparing AllRules and RAG results: under AllRules the model is given the full rule document, while under RAG it must retrieve the relevant parts itself, so the two conditions test different things.

\section{What the Applications Reveal About MESA}

    \subsection{Pilot Disagreements and Revisions}

    The two draft Summary of Ratings tables differ on seven of 21 section judgments: four for HLE, two for ARC-AGI-2 and one for DesignQA. This count describes the available drafts; it is not a human agreement estimate. The editor's final decision is also not an independent correctness label.

    In ARC-AGI-2 item 9.2.1, one draft assigned 2 while citing configurable run settings; the other assigned 1. Neither description supplies repeated-run results. The revised item states that temperatures, seeds and allowed attempts do not themselves measure stability. Where no usable study is available, the item is unknown (0); a result showing instability can justify 1 for a use requiring stable comparisons. Overall use limits must be explained separately.

    The analysis also found stronger conclusions than missing information warrants. The final HLE review calls small ranking differences statistically meaningless, and the DesignQA review describes small gaps as noise. Without a suitable comparison analysis, the safer conclusion is that their significance remains unresolved. These statements remain in the dated review files but are not adopted as demonstrated findings here.

    \emph{Answering RQ2}, the drafts expose unclear distinctions between settings and stability, between item ratings and overall use limits, and between different intended uses. Source references and positive findings, such as DesignQA's bounded diagnostic use, show useful output forms. The revision adds a short guide and shared source notes. No human timing data were collected, so the workload part of RQ2 remains unanswered.

    \subsection{Focused Checks of the Revised Instructions}

    Four fictional examples examined a broader claim on unchanged tasks, added validation of an answer grader, absent versus adverse stability results, and a supported narrow claim. The assistant's readings followed the expected distinctions: a science quiz alone did not justify general reasoning; grader validation supported scoring rather than intelligence; missing repeat-run evidence led to 0 while observed ranking reversals justified 1 for a stable-ranking claim; and a verified fixed-quiz score could be described without demanding broad transfer evidence. These examples were designed and judged by the same assistant and provide only limited checks of the wording.

    Two additional source packets exercised selected questions. HumanEval's documented executable scoring and pass@$k$ reporting required specifying the sampling and scoring conditions \cite{human_eval_repository}. TruthfulQA's generation and multiple-choice routes required different scorer checks and explicit version labels \cite{truthfulqa_repository}. These were readings of official README sections and original paper abstracts, not full benchmark audits or reproduced experiments. Both benchmarks are outside the 15-case construction inventory, but the assistant had prior familiarity with them.

    A limit emerged in the short form: it omits Section 9.3, including the model-grader question relevant to the TruthfulQA generation route. The guide refers the reviewer to the full template. That referral has not been tested with unfamiliar users. \emph{Answering RQ3}, selected questions behaved as intended in these readings, while the scorecard alone could not cover the scorer check. Full applications and independent review are still required before extending this finding to MESA as a whole.

\section{Discussion}

    MESA reframes benchmark use as a requirements traceability problem. If a project requirement states that an AI system must reason reliably, answer expert-level questions, or act autonomously, a benchmark score should not be treated as direct proof of an AI model's capability until the benchmark's target phenomenon, task sample, metric, execution conditions, baselines, uncertainty, and reporting claims have been reviewed. MESA supplies a structure for that review.
    
    For benchmark selection and acceptance criteria, MESA helps requirements engineers compare not only model scores but the materials and validity support behind them: a benchmark with clear materials, uncertainty reporting, scorer validation, contamination controls, and bounded claims may serve assurance better than a more visible one with ambiguous interpretation. It encourages teams to specify the score conditions enumerated above --- benchmark version, split, prompt, harness, scorer, comparator cohort, uncertainty, and run conditions --- together with the non-use caveats, instead of naming only a leaderboard score.
    
    Benchmark review is relevant when scores are proposed as support for consequential requirements. MESA offers a way to organize that review, but the present evaluation does not establish it as a required quality gate or a sufficient basis for safety, procurement or policy decisions.
    
    The development cases suggest recurring questions about score definitions, reference points, uncertainty, scorer validation and exposure to benchmark items. They do not establish universal minimum requirements. Model-grader checks apply when another model scores answers; human comparisons are especially relevant when human-level claims are made. Requirements should follow the intended interpretation.
    
    \emph{MESA's main contribution} is the explained adaptation of an established review structure to AI benchmarks. Separating facts from judgments and requiring reasons can make a review easier to inspect. Whether these requirements reduce errors or improve decisions compared with other approaches remains untested.
    
    Supported-use statements, source records and lists of missing information are proposed outputs for requirements work. Their practical value should be assessed by the people who would use them. The present examples establish neither certification readiness nor improved downstream decisions.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\label{sec:limitations}

    \subsection{Construct Validity}

        The source mapping explains why questions were included, but it does not prove that they cover every relevant benchmark property. It was prepared after the original construction and includes unresolved choices, such as how much item-structure evidence MESA should require. Familiarity with EFPA and the pilot cases may favor the adopted design over alternatives.

    \subsection{Internal Validity}

        The same AI assistant helped revise the instructions and assessed the new examples. The expected results were written before the results were recorded, but the examples were not blind or independently judged. The historical AI drafts may share errors, and reconciliation does not establish correctness. No causal effect of the revision on rating agreement or review speed was measured.
    
    \subsection{External Validity}
    
        The three original cases helped shape MESA. The two additional cases examine selected questions, not the complete workflow. These observations cannot establish performance across other benchmark families or other reviewers. Human completion time, training needs and rating agreement remain unknown.
    
    \subsection{Artifact Evolution}

        The original reviews identify an earlier 89-item template, whereas the current full template has 87 rated items. Some review text also uses inconsistent version labels, so the exact historical template commits are unresolved. The original files are preserved. New records identify the tested files by hashes, and historical ratings have not been transferred to the revised instructions. Source pages accessed without fixed repository commits may later change.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

    The next empirical step is a full application by the author with recorded time for finding sources, making judgments and writing. Other reviewers should then test clarity and agreement using the same source materials before comparison with unrestricted source gathering. Expert review could examine missing questions and disputed EFPA adaptations. A separate comparison would be needed to claim that MESA improves reviews over other methods.

    The repository already provides a 40-question scorecard and an interactive form. Their workload and ability to retain important findings need testing. In particular, the scorer checks omitted from the scorecard need a clearer tested route to the full template. Evaluating the AI drafting and editing process against human work remains a separate study.

\section{Conclusion}

    MESA adapts EFPA's descriptive and evaluative review structure to AI benchmark claims. The source map explains the current questions, and analysis of existing reviews identifies unclear rating decisions and unsupported inferences. The revised instructions separate missing studies from observed defects and ask reviewers to state the use being judged.

    Focused fictional and real examples illustrate those distinctions and reveal that the scorecard needs the full template for some scorer checks. These AI-assisted readings support continued development, not a claim that MESA is independently validated. Full applications, human workload observations and studies with other reviewers remain necessary to establish how well it serves its intended users.
