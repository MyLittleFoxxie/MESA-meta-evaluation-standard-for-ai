\begin{abstract}
Requirements Engineering (RE) for AI relies heavily on benchmark scores to justify model selection, safety claims, and procurement. However, these benchmarks are rarely audited as measurement instruments with clearly defined standards, explicit limits, and construct validity arguments. This paper introduces the Meta-Evaluation Standard for AI Benchmarks (MESA), a requirements-oriented framework for evaluating AI benchmarks before using their scores to substantiate capability claims. Crucially, MESA addresses a gap in trustworthy AI, in the sense that unrevised benchmarks used to evaluate AI models leave safety, fairness, and reliability claims empirically unverified. Drawing from the psychometric model of the EFPA's (European Federation of Psychologists' Associations) test review model; MESA features a two-part structure: Part A (Descriptive) catalogs the benchmark's design and administration, while Part B (Evaluative) assesses whether scores warrant the intended interpretations. We apply MESA to three contrasting benchmarks—Humanity's Last Exam (HLE), ARC-AGI-2, and DesignQA—to demonstrate how MESA surfaces evidence gaps and claim-proportionality risks in AI capability assessments. Our analysis revealed that HLE is best characterized as a difficult closed-ended academic question-answering benchmark, rather than a standalone basis for high-stakes ranking or broad intelligence claims; ARC-AGI-2 is best characterized as an exact-grid abstraction benchmark under specified evaluation conditions, rather than a standalone test of broad intelligence; and DesignQA is best characterized as a document-grounded engineering-requirements question-answering benchmark whose limited claims are offset by weak uncertainty reporting and a public, tunable test set. Ultimately, MESA provides an open-source, and replicable quality standard to help AI requirements engineers specify, audit, and validate the benchmark-based claims that underpin model selection, safety assessment, and procurement decisions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

    Benchmarks are one of the main mechanisms by which AI systems are compared, ranked, marketed, and interpreted. In practice, benchmark scores are often treated as direct indicators of capability and reused as evidence for model selection, procurement, safety claims, and public narratives about what AI systems can do. However, a benchmark score is not a capability itself. It is an inference drawn from a designed measurement instrument that operates under assumptions about content, administration, scoring, comparison, and interpretation. Benchmark quality is therefore central to whether AI model capability claims should be trusted.
    
    Recent research has identified multiple weaknesses in AI benchmarking practice, including benchmark contamination (i.e., test items leaking into model training data), prompt sensitivity (where scores varying significantly when prompt wording changes), saturation (i.e., top models scoring near the ceiling, leaving insufficient room to discriminate between them), over-interpretation of leaderboard rankings beyond what the underlying numbers support, unclear definitions of what the benchmark is supposed to measure, weak documentation, and poor reproducibility \cite{jo_wilson_what_does_your_benchmark_measure}. These problems threaten construct validity, where a model may score well because the benchmark is narrow, fragile, leaky, or poorly governed rather than because the model is genuinely capable.
    
    This paper argues that AI benchmarks should be reviewed with the same rigor as measurement instruments used to evaluate human cognitive ability. In human assessment, mature disciplines do not only score people on tests; they also review the tests themselves. The EFPA \textit{Test Review Model}, informed by the \textit{Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing}, offers a structured template for examining whether a test's content, scoring, evidence base, and interpretive claims are justified \cite{aera_apa_ncme_standards_2014,efpa_test_review_model_2025}. AI benchmarking lacks a similarly established review practice, despite the fact that benchmark results increasingly shape research agendas, procurement decisions, public claims about AI capabilities, and policy debates.
    
    The core operational requirement for benchmark use is \textit{traceability}, which reflects that a score interpretation should be traceable from claimed capability to task design, metric, score, comparison basis, and intended use. A score, however, is only as trustworthy as the measurement system that produced it, and that system's tasks, prompts, access conditions, scoring rules, baselines, and reporting surfaces may be poorly specified, unstable, or misaligned with the capability claim it is being used to support \cite{singh_leaderboard_illusion,line_goes_up}, silently propagating undetected measurement weaknesses into safety, fairness, and reliability decisions. This is already observable in software and requirements-engineering practice, where benchmarks that increasingly define agentic-system requirements --- scoring whether an agent can resolve repository-level issues, operate a terminal, or solve competitive programming tasks --- become the verification-and-validation evidence cited to accept or reject a requirement, yet rarely undergo the same scrutiny themselves.
    
    To address this gap, this paper proposes \textbf{MESA} (\textit{Meta-Evaluation Standard for AI Benchmarks}), a requirements-oriented framework for auditing AI benchmarks as measurement instruments. MESA's role is to help ensure that the test suites supplying model capability evidence are themselves robust, reproducible, and free from execution or contamination bias, making benchmark auditing a prerequisite for---rather than a substitute for---requirements verification. It is not another model-scoring benchmark. Rather, it is a framework for reviewing benchmarks themselves before their scores are used as evidence. MESA draws on three sources: it (1) adapts the EFPA Test Review Model's instrument description and evaluation, (2) incorporates benchmark-quality concerns \cite{reuel_betterbench} including documentation, reproducibility, implementation transparency, and lifecycle maintenance, and (3) applies Cattell-Horn-Carroll theory inspired construct-validity and capability-area perspectives to discipline the inferential chain from phenomenon to task, metric, score, and claim \cite{bean_measuring_what_matters,hendrycks_definition_agi}.
    
    With this, the paper aims to address three research questions:
    \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
        \item \textbf{RQ1}: How can the EFPA Test Review Model be adapted into an RE-oriented quality model for benchmark-based AI capability claims?
        \item \textbf{RQ2}: What benchmark materials and construct validity support are needed for trustworthy AI benchmark score interpretation?
        \item \textbf{RQ3}: What interpretation risks and evidence gaps does MESA reveal across three contrasting AI benchmarks: Humanity's Last Exam, ARC-AGI-2, and DesignQA?
    \end{enumerate}
    
    MESA helps requirements engineers, benchmark users, benchmark maintainers, and AI assurance stakeholders judge which benchmark claims are supported, which are evidence gaps, and how benchmark scores should or should not be used.

\section{Related Work}

    \subsection{AI Benchmarking Practices and Challenges}
    
        Concerns across the AI benchmarking literature cluster around two questions: what a benchmark actually measures and how its scores are used, such that a recurring concern is the validity and scope of evaluations \cite{alonso_church_sigir}. Critiques find that popular benchmarks are often narrow, fragile, or unclear about their target construct, and that headline numbers can reflect saturation or shortcut exploitation rather than the capability they are taken to indicate \cite{jo_wilson_what_does_your_benchmark_measure,line_goes_up}. Regarding how scores are used, work on leaderboard practices documents systematic over-interpretation and distortion --- selective comparison, unreported evaluation conditions, and ranking instability --- that make leaderboard positions weaker evidence than their public presentation suggests \cite{singh_leaderboard_illusion}. Contamination and selective reporting worsen the problem: as benchmarks age and leak into training corpora, memorization inflates scores while competitive pressure incentivizes gaming and cherry-picking \cite{cheng_benchmarking_broken}. A related failure is benchmark drift, where an instrument is repurposed beyond its original design --- as when the Penn Treebank \cite{marcus_etal_1993_building}, built for syntactic parsing, was later reused as a perplexity benchmark in ways that detached the metric from its intended meaning.
        
        In response, the community has emphasized documentation and standardization. Datasheets for datasets and model cards establish structured reporting of provenance, intended use, and limitations \cite{gebru_datasheets}, while others score benchmarks against best-practice criteria spanning design, implementation, documentation, and maintenance \cite{reuel_betterbench}. These efforts are valuable but largely \emph{developer-facing} and artifact-level: they help creators build and document better benchmarks rather than help a downstream reviewer decide what an existing benchmark's scores are permitted to mean. MESA builds on and expands this literature by unifying these threads into a single, \emph{reviewer-facing} instrument-review template that separates factual description from evaluative judgment and disciplines the inferential chain from phenomenon to task to metric to claim. The gap MESA addresses is that AI benchmarks are widely used as measurement instruments, yet are rarely reviewed with a unified structure that ties every judgment to evidence, missing evidence, and a bounded statement of supported and non-supported use.
    
    \subsection{Benchmarking standards across the fields}
    
        Beyond the AI benchmarking literature, MESA is informed by review and measurement practices from adjacent fields --- requirements engineering, psychometric test review, construct-validity theory, and cognitive-ability research --- and we identify common themes regarding what makes a measurement instrument trustworthy. Where applicable, we adapt these practices into the MESA Test Review Model.
        
        \textbf{Requirements engineering for AI systems.} RE research emphasizes that AI development changes the subjects and practices of requirements work. Habiba et al. \cite{habiba_re4ai_maturity_2024} provide a systematic mapping of 126 primary studies, showing growth around elicitation and analysis but persistent challenges in specification, validation, and quality requirements. This motivates MESA's framing of benchmark claims, tasks, scoring rules, and reproducibility materials as requirements-relevant review objects rather than neutral background infrastructure.
        
        \textbf{Psychometric instrument review.} The EFPA Test Review Model \cite{efpa_test_review_model_2025} evaluates psychological and educational instruments in two parts: a descriptive part (identity, users, administration, scoring, reports) and an evaluative part (rationale, materials, norms, reliability, validity, fair use, and final interpretation), and cautions against mechanically averaging ratings where expert judgment is required. MESA adapts this structure because a benchmark score, like a test score, is not self-justifying: it requires evidence that the instrument supports the meaning users attach to it.
        
        \textbf{Construct validity and the phenomenon--task--metric--claim chain.} Bean et al. \cite{bean_measuring_what_matters} supply a psychometric validity lens, framing benchmark validity as a chain from the real-world phenomenon, through the chosen task and scoring metric, to the claim made about the score. This chain maps naturally onto requirements traceability, and MESA uses it to test whether a score is valid evidence for the requirement-like claim attached to it.
        
        \textbf{Broad-intelligence and AGI claims.} Hendrycks et al. \cite{hendrycks_definition_agi} confront the strongest benchmark claims --- those equating performance with general intelligence --- grounding AGI in Cattell--Horn--Carroll (CHC) theory as a breadth-weighted profile across cognitive domains, and reporting a 'jagged' profile in current models that overstates general competence. MESA draws the lesson of claim proportionality: strong performance on one task family is narrow task-level evidence unless a validity argument spans the relevant abilities. MESA does not require every benchmark to be a full cognitive battery and acknowledges that CHC interpretation remains debated \cite{canivez_chc_critique}.

\section{Methodology}

The objective is to establish a rigorous and replicable review model for auditing benchmark design, materials, score interpretations, and evidence gaps.     \subsection{Framework Construction}
    
        MESA was derived to make the benchmark itself a review object. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:mesa-methodology-pipeline}, 15 benchmarks acted as input sources to help form a structural model adapted from the EFPA Test Review Model. The adaptation was functional rather than textual, following four rules: \emph{preserve} fields whose review function transfers directly to AI benchmarking; \emph{rename} fields when the function transfers but where the previous human-centric terminology is misleading; \emph{replace} fields when the original assumes human-assessment conditions incompatible with AI benchmarks; and \emph{add} AI-specific fields where the EFPA model would otherwise overlook a material benchmark dependency. For example, the EFPA test taker maps to the evaluated AI system and the target population to the intended AI systems or model class; psychometric norms expand into technical reference points (baselines, task comparators, chance floors, human or expert references, model cohorts, and criterion thresholds); and digitally generated reports translate into benchmark papers, result files, dashboards, scorecards, and leaderboards. The added fields incorporate modern engineering parameters such as prompt templates, hidden data splits, scorer code execution, model-as-judge behaviors, API runtime conditions, contamination controls, software versioning, and leaderboard governance.
    
    \subsection{Adaptation and Evidence Integration}
    
        The structural adaptation followed four foundational rules. First, preserve fields whose underlying review function transfers directly to AI benchmarking. Second, rename fields when the core function transfers but the human-centric terminology is misleading in the context of AI. Third, replace fields when the original framework assumes human-assessment conditions that are incompatible with AI benchmarks. Fourth, add AI-specific fields where the EFPA model would otherwise overlook a material benchmark dependency. These new additions incorporate modern engineering parameters, including prompt templates, hidden data splits, scorer code execution, model-as-judge behaviors, API runtime conditions, contamination controls, software versioning, leaderboard governance, and more.
        
        AI benchmark-quality literature was then integrated into the EFPA-derived structure. Reuel et al. \cite{reuel_betterbench} informed lifecycle, reproducibility, implementation, documentation, maintenance, and contamination dimensions. Bean et al. \cite{bean_measuring_what_matters} informed the phenomenon-task-metric-claim chain. AGI and CHC literature informed the review of broad cognitive claims \cite{hendrycks_definition_agi}.
    
    \subsection{Intended Empirical Validation Strategy}
    
        The present study establishes feasibility and coverage through the structured, LLM-assisted case studies described in Section \ref{sec:demonstration}; it does not yet validate MESA as a measurement instrument. The intended validation strategy centers on human-in-the-loop consensus panels: independent requirements engineers, benchmark developers, model evaluators, and domain experts would apply MESA to a shared benchmark cohort, allowing us to estimate inter-rater agreement on Part B ratings, quantify the effect of reviewer training, and compare reconciled human panels against the multi-LLM pipeline. This planned study is detailed as the primary thread of future work (Section \ref{sec:future}).
    
\section{The MESA Test Review Model}
    
    The MESA Test Review Model treats an AI benchmark as both a requirements object and a source of score-based claims. The review model has two main parts: Part A describes the benchmark and its materials, while Part B evaluates whether the benchmark supports the interpretations users are expected to draw from its scores. Table~\ref{tab:mesa-review-structure} summarizes the high-level structure of the full review model.

    \paragraph{Part A, Description} Part A asks what the benchmark is before judging whether it is good, recording the factual description, classification, scoring, outputs, and access/lifecycle material catalogued in Sections~1--5 of Table~\ref{tab:mesa-review-structure}. Crucially, Part A does not presuppose that a benchmark is fully documented. Many benchmarks publish only a paper, a leaderboard, or a bare dataset; where a descriptive field has no available evidence, Part A records it as an explicit 'unknown' evidence gap rather than leaving it blank or inferring a value, so that the subsequent Part B evaluation can distinguish a genuine defect from missing documentation.
    
    \paragraph{Part B, Evaluation} Part B asks whether the benchmark supports its intended interpretation, preserving EFPA's evaluative categories (Sections~6--12 of Table~\ref{tab:mesa-review-structure}) while translating them for AI benchmarks. The translation foregrounds concerns specific to computational instruments: reliability across runs, prompts, scorers, and judges (Section~9); and validity evidence spanning contamination, gameability---how easily a system can score high without actually having the targeted ability---and claim proportionality (Section~10).
    
    MESA uses EFPA-style ratings of \texttt{n/a} and \texttt{0}--\texttt{4}. A \texttt{0} means that the reviewer cannot rate the attribute because information is missing or insufficient. A \texttt{1} means the available evidence is inadequate for the stated purpose. This distinction is central for AI benchmarks: missing documentation is an evidence gap, not automatically a demonstrated defect. The final evaluation does not just report ratings. It produces supported-use and non-use statements: a concise account of what the score can be used to claim, what it should not be used to claim, and what conditions must be held fixed for comparisons to be meaningful, such as benchmark version, split, prompt or harness, scorer, comparator cohort, uncertainty, and run conditions. This replaces mechanical averaging of ratings with a traceable statement of how the score may be used.
    
    \paragraph{Template coherence} The template contains deliberate overlap because the same benchmark material can serve different review functions. A prompt template can be recorded in Part A as benchmark material, evaluated in Section 7 for usability, examined in Section 9 as a source of score instability, assessed in Section 10 as a validity threat, and reported in Section 12 as a condition for leaderboard interpretation. This is not redundant if each section preserves its role: description, material usability, reliability, validity, fairness, and communication. The same principle applies to other cross-cutting artifacts, such as hidden test sets, scorer code, and contamination controls.
    
    \paragraph{MESA as its own measurement object} MESA can be conceptualized through the very phenomenon-task-metric-claim chain it uses to evaluate other instruments. In this meta-evaluation context, the phenomenon of interest is whether a benchmark’s score interpretations are defensible for Requirements Engineering (RE) use. The task is the execution of the structured review itself—including compiling the source inventory, completing Part A descriptions, grounding Part B ratings in evidence, and rendering a final summary judgment. The resulting metric consists of EFPA-style ratings (N/A and 0–4) coupled with qualitative reviewer evidence and cautions, intentionally rejecting an oversimplified aggregate score. Consequently, the claim MESA is licensed to make remains deliberately narrow: it supports source-grounded statements regarding whether benchmark scores can carry a stated interpretation, rather than a generic numeric quality score for the benchmark as a whole. This framing explicitly bounds MESA's measurement limits, preventing readers from misconstruing individual section ratings as holistic quality verdicts.
    
    \paragraph{Scope of MESA judgments} Two boundary conditions follow from this framing. First, MESA reviews the \emph{instrument}, not the test-taker. The interpretation of a score is codependent on the model that produced it: a text-only model that scores well on ARC-AGI-2's grids licenses a stronger generalization claim than one pre-trained on similar layouts, and a high score from a model that may have memorized a public dataset licenses almost none. A complete capability argument therefore combines a MESA review of the benchmark with model-side evidence---training data, prior capabilities, and runtime scaffolding, as disclosed in artifacts such as Model Cards \cite{mitchell_model_cards_2019}---and with the intended use; MESA supplies a necessary component of that argument, not the whole of it. Second, the multi-LLM pipeline used in our case studies (Section \ref{sec:demonstration}) is an assistive drafting and schema-mapping tool, not a substitute for expert judgment. For high-stakes assurance cases, safety certifications, or procurement decisions, MESA reviews should be adjudicated by human expert panels; the framework structures and accelerates that review rather than automating it away.

\section{Case Study Design}
\label{sec:demonstration}

    MESA was applied to three benchmarks selected to vary in task format, scoring procedure, and public framing: Humanity's Last Exam (HLE), ARC-AGI-2, and DesignQA. HLE is an expert-level academic question-answering benchmark with multiple-choice and short-answer items, including a partially multimodal subset; it uses a model-as-judge procedure to assess answer equivalence \cite{phan_etal_hle_2025}. ARC-AGI-2 consists of visual grid-transformation tasks with public tasks and hidden evaluation splits; it is scored by exact cell-level grid matching and is publicly framed around progress toward general intelligence \cite{chollet_arc_agi_2_2025}. DesignQA evaluates question answering over an engineering rule document and CAD-derived data; it uses lexical and semantic similarity metrics, including F1, BLEU-2, ROUGE-L, and Sentence-BERT, and is framed more narrowly as an engineering-documentation benchmark rather than an AGI benchmark \cite{doris_designqa_2024}.
    
    \begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/mesa-test-case-pipeline.png}
    \caption{Case study review pipeline. Each benchmark's information sources (HLE, ARC-AGI-2, or DesignQA) were paired with the MESA Test Review Model and sent to two independent reviewer models. A supervising editor model reconciled the resulting MESA by removing discrepancies to produce final MESA evaluations.}
    \label{fig:mesa-test-case-pipeline}
    \end{figure*}
    
    To reduce single-reviewer bias, each case followed the multi-LLM pipeline in Figure~\ref{fig:mesa-test-case-pipeline}. Two reviewer models (Reviewer~1, ChatGPT~5.5; Reviewer~2, Claude Opus~4.7) ran the same benchmark-reviewer specification on the same per-benchmark inputs and produced MESA drafts without seeing each other's output. A supervising editor (Gemini~3.1~Pro) then reconciled each discrepancy---in Part A facts, Part B ratings, and Part B rationales---against the sources, and the reconciled review was checked for source grounding, descriptive--evaluative separation, and reviewer cautions before being recorded as the case output. This is a feasibility demonstration of the workflow, not a validation study (Sections~\ref{sec:threats} and~\ref{sec:future}).
    
    \paragraph{Worked example} For HLE Section 9 (Reliability), Reviewer~1 rated 2 and Reviewer~2 rated 1; the editor set the final rating to 1 using the four-element anchoring model. The \textbf{evidence} is a Wald confidence interval in the scoring harness and a 15--18\% expert disagreement rate in the paper. The \textbf{missing evidence} is run-to-run stability, prompt-sensitivity analysis, and validation of the \texttt{o3-mini} judge against human graders, with leaderboard point estimates that omit uncertainty. The \textbf{editor rationale} is that, for a leaderboard ranking models by thin margins, absent uncertainty and an unvalidated judge are reliability gaps, not missing documentation. The \textbf{supported-use and non-use statement} is that HLE results give a coarse performance summary but do not support position-by-position rankings. The anchor is this four-element profile, not the number.

\section{Case Study Results}

    \subsection{Humanity's Last Exam (HLE)}
    
        MESA reviewed the static HLE release: 2,500 public questions, a documented private held-out split, the scoring and evaluation code, and the public leaderboards. HLE covers multiple-choice and short-answer formats with partial image-conditioned items, automated grading with model-as-judge support for equivalent answers, and metrics including accuracy, category-level performance, and calibration error\footnote{Calibration error measures the gap between a model's stated confidence and its accuracy.} \cite{phan_etal_hle_2025}. Item development was expert-led, the dataset and scoring materials are open, and confidence logging exposes miscalibration. Because model-based judging carries measurement risk, MESA treats scorer validation as core evidence \cite{cheng_benchmarking_broken}.

        MESA also finds gaps that bound those scores. The default judge (\texttt{o3-mini}) is not validated against human judges; the paper reports a 15--18\% expert disagreement rate, and an independent chemistry and biology reanalysis estimates errors up to 30\% in some text-only subjects \cite{skarlinski_futurehouse_hle_2025}, which bounds achievable scores. Run-to-run variance is unpublished for stochastic models, and no human baseline was run under the benchmark's own constraints (no internet or external tools), so the 'expert-level' framing is ungrounded. The public leaderboard\footnote{Public leaderboard available at \url{https://agi.safe.ai}, accessed 2026-05-18.} drops the paper's non-AGI caveats and reports decimal-precision ranks without confidence intervals.
        
        MESA therefore supports using HLE to measure performance on closed-ended academic tasks (including some multimodal inputs) under the official prompting setup, and to diagnose overconfidence and miscalibration. It does not support using HLE alone to justify fine-grained leaderboard rankings or 'expert-equivalent' claims, nor as evidence of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), autonomous research, open-ended scientific creativity, general cognitive versatility, or long-horizon planning\footnote{Long-horizon planning refers to multi-step tasks where an agent plans and executes actions across many consecutive turns.}.
    
    \subsection{ARC-AGI-2}
    
        MESA reviewed the ARC-AGI-2 ecosystem: public training and evaluation task files, documented semi-private and private evaluation splits, and public leaderboards. Systems infer a generative rule from a few demonstration input-output pairs and must produce exact output grids for unseen test inputs \cite{chollet_arc_agi_2_2025}. The task design uses a fixed format with deterministic exact-cell scoring and open public tasks. It includes a human reference point from a 407-participant calibration campaign (515 sessions, 13{,}405 attempts) requiring at least two independent human solves per task, contamination controls via secure semi-private and private splits backed by an offline Kaggle sandbox, and cost-per-task reporting.

        Its weaknesses are statistical and conceptual. The materials frame the benchmark as measuring AGI, psychometric intelligence, and fluid intelligence\footnote{Fluid intelligence refers to the capacity to solve novel problems without relying on prior knowledge or language skills.}, yet the task family omits language, world knowledge, memory, planning, social cognition, embodiment, tool use, long-horizon agency, auditory processing, and real-world transfer. With 120 tasks in the public and semi-private splits, a 1\% delta is about one task, yet headline scores carry no confidence intervals, standard errors, or repeat-run variance, so small ranking deltas are indistinguishable from noise. The leaderboard also mixes unlabeled runtime environments---the Kaggle offline sandbox (12-hour budget on $4\times$ NVIDIA L4 GPUs) and hosted APIs with cost-bounded scaffolding\footnote{Scaffolding refers to the orchestration code, agentic loops, retrieval systems, and tool-access layers wrapped around the model.}---which limits cross-system comparison. A documented 2-versus-3 trial inconsistency with the official pass@2 rule\footnote{Under pass@2, a task is solved if the model produces the correct grid in at most two attempts.} and absent per-task category labels for the four core challenge types block analysis of whether the sub-categories form a coherent construct.
        
        MESA therefore supports using ARC-AGI-2 to measure exact-grid abstraction and rule-induction under specified split, attempt, verification, and compute-cost conditions, with system-to-system comparison valid only when systems run under identical environment and scaffolding constraints. It does not support standalone claims of AGI preparedness, human-level versatility, or broad psychometric intelligence; it is AGI-relevant only as one narrow component within a larger evaluation portfolio.
        
        MESA reviewed the public DesignQA materials \cite{doris_designqa_2024}: the dataset, the GitHub repository (\texttt{anniedoris/design\_qa}), and a manually updated leaderboard. Multimodal models answer 1,451 question-answer pairs across six subsets (Retrieval, Compilation, Definition, Presence, Dimension, and Functional Performance) drawn from the Formula SAE (FSAE) 2024 rule document and MIT Motorsports CAD data, under a full-context (AllRules) or retrieval-augmented (RAG) condition. It is the most requirements-engineering-focused of the three cases. It uses real source materials, expert authoring with non-author review, an error analysis, and a paper that states its limits and makes no AGI claims.
    
    \subsection{DesignQA}

        MESA also identifies important gaps in reliability, validation, and maintenance. DesignQA reports point estimates without confidence intervals or repeated-run evidence for the main model comparisons. This matters most for the smaller subsets: Functional Performance contains only 16 items, so a single item changes accuracy by 6.25 percentage points. The benchmark is also fully public, and the paper discusses fine-tuning on Retrieval QAs, which would amount to training on released test items. Scoring introduces additional validity limits because it depends heavily on response format and wording. Strict format matching, single reference answers, and BLEU/ROUGE length effects can penalize answers that are semantically acceptable but phrased differently. Finally, the inspected materials do not provide a human baseline, a visible `LICENSE` file, or versioned releases.
        
        MESA therefore supports using DesignQA as a diagnostic benchmark for multimodal models on Formula SAE requirements QA, especially when results are reported by subset and the context condition is held fixed. Small subset differences, roughly below 10--15 percentage points, should be treated cautiously unless uncertainty evidence is added. The review does not support fine-grained model ranking, direct comparison by the simple-average score, procurement use, or broad claims about engineering competence. It also cautions against directly comparing AllRules and RAG results, because those conditions test different mixtures of model capability and context-retrieval support.

\section{Discussion}

    MESA reframes benchmark use as a requirements traceability problem. If a project requirement states that an AI system must reason reliably, answer expert-level questions or act autonomously, a benchmark score should not be treated as direct proof of an AI model's capability until the benchmark's target phenomenon, task sample, metric, execution conditions, baselines, uncertainty, and reporting claims have been reviewed. MESA supplies a structure for that review.
    
    For benchmark selection and acceptance criteria, MESA helps requirements engineers compare not only model scores but the materials and validity support behind them: a benchmark with clear materials, uncertainty reporting, scorer validation, contamination controls, and bounded claims may serve assurance better than a more visible one with ambiguous interpretation. It encourages teams to specify the benchmark version, split, prompt or harness, scorer, threshold, comparator, and non-use caveats instead of naming only a leaderboard score.
    
    This matters most for trustworthy AI, where fairness, robustness, reliability, and capability requirements increasingly depend on benchmark scores in both procurement and emerging regulatory frameworks. MESA asks whether those tests are reproducible, comparable, accessible, maintained, and valid for the intended claim. By making the gap between benchmark scope and capability claims explicit and traceable, it functions as a requirements-level quality gate: not a replacement for domain-specific safety analysis, but a structured precondition for treating any benchmark-based result as trustworthy evidence.
    
    \emph{Answering RQ2}, the three test applications converged on a minimum set of criteria before benchmark scores can be responsibly reused as RE evidence: a precisely defined target phenomenon with explicit non-use caveats; transparent scoring rules backed by empirical model-as-judge validation; chance, human, and expert reference points under identical evaluation conditions; published uncertainty metrics (confidence intervals, run-to-run variance, prompt sensitivity); and contamination controls, version tracking, and bounded reporting surfaces that show core limitations next to headline scores. An instrument lacking these elements can still be useful, but its outputs must be read as task-level.
    
    \emph{MESA's main contribution} is the disciplined separation of benchmark description from benchmark evaluation. In ordinary discourse, descriptions such as hard, new, broad, or popular quietly become quality judgments; MESA blocks that shortcut by asking first what the benchmark claims, how it operates, and what materials and score interpretations it offers, and only then whether the evidence is sufficient. This guards against two errors: unsupported criticism, where missing documentation is taken as proof a benchmark is poor (MESA treats it as an evidence gap whose weight depends on intended use), and unsupported endorsement, where difficulty, popularity, or leaderboard visibility is taken as proof of validity (MESA requires a validity argument connecting phenomenon, task, metric, and claim).
    
    Beyond individual audits, MESA contributes a reusable vocabulary for RE practice. The concepts of evidence gaps, claim-proportionality risks, supported-use and non-use statements, and score-condition specifications give requirements engineers precise language to document what a benchmark can and cannot certify. This vocabulary can be embedded directly into requirements specifications, procurement criteria, system assurance cases, and AI governance documentation — making benchmark-based claims first-class, traceable artifacts in the RE process rather than informal footnotes.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\label{sec:limitations}

    \subsection{Construct Validity}

        Construct threats concern whether MESA actually measures the quality of an AI benchmark as a measurement instrument. We mitigate this risk by grounding MESA's structure in the EFPA Test Review Model \cite{efpa_test_review_model_2025}, refined over decades of educational and psychological testing, and by integrating concrete software-engineering criteria from BetterBench \cite{reuel_betterbench} and workload-relevance principles from Alonso and Church \cite{alonso_church_sigir}. MESA nonetheless inherits the contested boundaries of these sources; for example, the mapping from psychometric categories to benchmark review involves judgment that another adaptation might make differently.

    \subsection{Internal Validity}

        Internal threats concern the subjectivity of MESA's categorical scoring---a reviewer might rate Section 9 (reliability) differently depending on their tolerance for variance. MESA constrains this by requiring every Part B rating to be justified by documented evidence or flagged as an explicit 'unknown' gap; ratings remain judgment-based by design, matching EFPA's logic. A second threat is generation bias from the LLM-drafted pilot reviews, mitigated by strict textual-grounding rules, independent dual drafting, and human-inspectable reconciliation---though the reconciled outputs may still share LLM blind spots, making the pipeline a feasibility demonstration rather than a substitute for human adjudication.
    
    \subsection{External Validity}
    
        External threats concern generalization. MESA was applied to three benchmarks chosen for contrast---HLE, ARC-AGI-2, and DesignQA--- and based of 15 benchmarks from widespread fields. While this is a very big span of possibilities, the ever-growing field of AI will no doubt lead to benchmarks that might fall out of the MESA's current scope.
    
    \subsection{Open-Source Artifact}
    
        The MESA model is maintained as an open-source Markdown document in the public repository, so this paper captures a snapshot of a framework that will continue to evolve; future applications should cite or pin the repository commit or access date used for a given review.
        
        Despite these threats, MESA offers an immediate, practical contribution: an open-source, replicable artifact that requirements engineers can apply today, letting limitations be addressed incrementally as they surface, without invalidating existing reviews.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

    Future work will validate MESA as a review instrument through multi-reviewer studies, inter-rater agreement analysis, structured reviewer training, and refinement of ambiguous rating anchors. The review process should also be evaluated with benchmark maintainers, requirements engineers, model evaluators, psychometric experts, and downstream users. A complementary line of work will validate the multi-LLM review pipeline itself: comparing reconciled LLM outputs against expert human reviewer panels, measuring LLM-reviewer agreement, analyzing sensitivity to the choice of reviewer and editor models, and characterizing the stability of MESA judgments under prompt perturbations.
    
    A second direction is tool support. A full MESA review is intentionally detailed, but many RE workflows need a compact scorecard that preserves evidence without hiding caveats. Future tooling could generate a benchmark requirements profile, supported-interpretation statement, evidence-gap list, and non-use statements from the full review.
    
    Finally, MESA should be applied to a larger benchmark sample. The aim should not be to rank benchmarks mechanically, but to identify recurring weaknesses in benchmark documentation, scoring validity, reliability, contamination controls, maintenance, and public communication.

\section{Conclusion}

    AI benchmark scores increasingly function as evidence in Requirements Engineering for AI systems, influencing model selection, acceptance criteria, assurance arguments, and public claims. MESA adapts the EFPA Test Review Model into a requirements-oriented review framework that preserves EFPA's rule of describing the benchmark before judging it, while integrating AI-specific concerns from benchmark-quality, construct-validity, and broad-intelligence literature. By asking reviewers to document benchmark facts before assigning evaluative ratings and to tie every judgment to evidence, missing evidence, and supported-use and non-use statements, MESA makes benchmark-based claims auditable rather than rhetorical. The HLE, ARC-AGI-2, and DesignQA case studies demonstrate that benchmark scores support narrower interpretations than their public framing or default use suggests. Making that distinction explicit, and traceable from phenomenon to task to metric to claim, is what an RE-oriented benchmark review framework should provide.
    
    For the trustworthy AI requirements engineering community specifically, MESA offers something more than a review checklist: it re-frames benchmark use as a requirements traceability problem. A trustworthiness claim backed by a benchmark score is only as strong as the measurement evidence behind that score. By making that evidence (and its gaps) visible, structured, open-source, and replicable, MESA equips requirements engineers, AI assurance practitioners, and policymakers with the tools to demand, and demonstrate, the evidentiary standard that responsible AI deployment requires.
