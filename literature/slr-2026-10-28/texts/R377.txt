Abstract
Artificial Intelligence (AI) models are increasingly developed, published and used across scientific fields. Researchers, students and
industry professionals who want to apply these models to their own research questions often lack the programming and data science
expertise to do so. Existing guidelines cover single steps, but not the route from a published model to working predictions on one’s
own data. Here we present the hands-on workflow PRAISE, a Practical, Reproducible AI-model Implementation for Science and
Education, that helps non-programmers implement published AI models step by step. Because implementations rarely work the first
time, PRAISE anticipates common failures and provides a fallback route for each. As a proof of principle, three BSc chemistry
students and their tutor applied PRAISE without prior programming or data science knowledge. They reproduced, validated and
extended a state-of-the-art model that predicts enzyme-substrate interactions. This shows that with PRAISE, implementing an AI
model from the literature becomes a learnable skill, one that turns non-programmer scientists from users into evaluators and improvers
of AI models.
Keywords: Artificial intelligence, higher education, AI literacy, model deployment, low-barrier AI adaptation, AI accessibility
1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a rapidly advanc￾ing field, generating an increasing number of applications with
societal relevance (Jumper et al., 2021; Sajadieh et al., 2026;
Yang et al., 2025). Researchers, students and industry profession￾als, e.g. in STEM disciplines, who want to apply published AI
models to a problem often encounter difficulties implementing
and operationalizing these models. Implementing a published
AI model in a new setting may be difficult for several reasons.
Professionals may lack data-science expertise or suitable com￾putational infrastructure. They may also be unfamiliar with the
installation, training, and validation of AI pipelines. Insufficient
AI literacy can further discourage professionals from initiating
the implementation process (Biagini, 2025; Pinski & Benlian,
2024). Bridging the gap between the conventional scientific
disciplines and data science is essential for useful and ethical AI
model implementation (Pion-Tonachini et al., 2021).
This paper presents PRAISE, a Practical, Reproducible AI￾model Implementation for Science and Education, a hands-on
workflow that helps non-programmer scientists with a repro￾ducible implementation of a published AI model to their own

practical application. Existing AI lifecycles and workflows de￾scribe the pipeline design, data preparation, training, validation,
and deployment (Amershi et al., 2019; International Organization

Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023; OECD, 2019),
however these workflows stop short of the practical require￾ments for implementing an existing, literature-derived model.
A different body of work addresses non-programmer scientists

published model to a working deployment is left undescribed.
To avoid any possible confusion, we fix some terminology
here that is used throughout this work: implementation is the
umbrella term for the whole route of selecting, installing, running
and validating an AI model. Reproduction indicates running the
published model and obtaining metrics comparable to those
reported. Adaptation means retraining the model on data the user
adds, and/or with changes in the model pipeline. Deployment,
the final stage of the AI life cycle (International Organization
for Standardization, 2022b; OECD, 2019), denotes making the
validated model available for new predictions in the use case, e.g.
as a script or web interface.

2. How an AI model learns from data, makes predictions
and is validated
A predictive AI model learns relationships between input
data and known outcomes during training, subsequently the
model uses these learned relationships to make predictions for
new data. The validity of this process depends on the quality of
the input data and how the neural network is trained. Evaluation
is essential to safeguard the validity. In this section the data,
training process and validation of the outcomes are discussed.
2.1. Data as the basis for learning
AI models need to be trained on relevant, fair and valid data.
For training to be successful, the dataset must fulfill several
requirements.
2.1.1. Dataset size
The dataset should be of sufficient size to represent the
underlying population. Small datasets may lead to overfitting,
reducing the reliability of predictions (Rajput et al., 2023).
Model performance generally improves with increasing dataset
size until a saturation point is reached (Rajput et al., 2023). What
constitutes a ‘small’ or ‘sufficiently large’ dataset depends on
data complexity, model architecture, and task, since learning is
more difficult when input data is highly variable or unstructured
(Naser, 2026). As an empirical rule, a minimum number of
samples per feature is often given, from 3 to 300 samples per
feature, dependent on the intended use (Naser, 2026).
2.1.2. Non-ideal dataset properties
Datasets may suffer from imperfections that affect the ap￾plicability and reliability of the resulting AI models. Non-ideal
data distributions take several forms:
Gaps in training data reduce the applicability and reliability
of a model. For example, an AI model trained to predict chemical
toxicity may perform poorly when certain categories of com￾pounds are underrepresented or absent from the dataset. Such
gaps reduce the applicability domain of the model and limit its
practical relevance (Hartung & Kleinstreuer, 2025).
Biased datasets contain features or categories that are dispro￾portionately represented relative to the true underlying population
(Eid et al., 2021). In a toxicology context, a dataset dominated
by molecules with similar physicochemical properties may un￾intentionally specialize the model toward those compounds,
causing performance to deteriorate for chemically distinct struc￾tures. J. Chen and Bao (2025) intentionally created biases in
a dataset about strength of concrete beams and found that this
compromised ML model accuracy and universality.
Skewness is an asymmetry in the data distribution (Monard
& Batista, 2002). In the case of the toxicology example, a
dataset containing 99% non-toxic chemicals and only 1% toxic
compounds is skewed towards the majority class. Such imbalance
is not necessarily unrealistic. Many real-world datasets, such as
fraudulent transaction detection datasets, naturally contain rare
minority classes (Monard & Batista, 2002). Increasing the dataset
size will disproportionately benefit minority class representation
(Weiss & Provost, 2001). Model performance increased when
the minority classes were represented in the dataset for 50%
instead of their true 19% presence (Weiss & Provost, 2001).
A related issue is the absence of confirmed negative data￾points. In many datasets, negatives are constructed by random
negative sampling, which can introduce false negatives into both
training and validation sets (Eid et al., 2021).
2.1.3. Dataset splitting
Before usage, the data are generally split into separate subsets
for testing, training and validation. This separation is important
because the model should be evaluated on data that were not used
to adjust its parameters. Conventional partitions are an 80:20 ratio
for a two-way train-test split and 64:16:20 for a train-validation￾test split, although no consensus exists on these proportions and
an optimal split depends on the number of parameters in a linear
regression model (Joseph, 2022). The partition itself is often
generated randomly and is therefore controlled by a random seed.
Reproducible splits can be made by recording and reporting the
seed (Lyu et al., 2021; Wegmeth et al., 2023).
Data leakage occurs when a model is trained on information
it should not have access to during training, often resulting in
unrealistically high model performance (Apicella et al., 2025).
The authors propose a data-leakage taxonomy, based on the place
in the pipeline where the data leakage occurs. In Section 4.2 a
leakage protocol checklist is proposed.
2.2. From input data to a prediction
Once a dataset has been assembled and split, the model
itself determines how the input variables are converted into
a prediction. This section describes the structure of a neural
network, how it learns through minimizing the loss function, and
which types of machine learning exist.
2.2.1. Structure of neural networks
A neural network consists of a chain of layers of nodes,
sometimes referred to as neurons. Besides the input and output
layers, the network may consist of several hidden layers in the
case of deep learning. The length of the chain gives the network
its depth.
Information is fed to the nodes of the input layer and then
moves from one layer to the next, until it reaches the output layer,
which gives the results of the network. Each layer receives the

previous layer’s outputs as inputs and passes its own outputs to
the subsequent layer. In this way, each consecutive hidden layer
applies a further transformation to the representation produced
by the preceding layers. This enables the network to transform
input data into a representation that facilitates modelling, while
optimizing its parameters to capture the underlying relationships
accurately (Goodfellow et al., 2016a). Each node multiplies its
inputs by weights, sums them, adds a bias, and passes the result
through a nonlinear activation function to produce its output
(Goodfellow et al., 2016a). This nonlinearity allows the network
to capture complex relationships in the data.
2.2.2. Loss function minimization
The structure of a neural network, i.e. the number of layers,
the number of nodes per layer and the activation functions, is
typically fixed from the outset, while the weights and biases are
optimized during training. In supervised learning, a loss function
quantifies the discrepancy between a neural network’s prediction
and the corresponding target value, thereby measuring prediction
error. A fully trained AI model has weights and biases such that
the loss function is minimal on the training set. An optimal set of
these parameters is obtained during the training of the network.
At the start of a training session, some typical values for
these parameters are chosen (randomly or with some background
knowledge). A single batch of the training dataset is then fed to
the neural network and the output is determined.
During backpropagation, the prediction error is propagated
backward through the network. Gradients are calculated for each
weight and bias, and an optimization algorithm uses them to
update these parameters and reduce the loss (Goodfellow et al.,
2016a; Narihira et al., 2021). The updated parameters are then
used in the next forward pass of the following batch. This process
of calculating a prediction, calculating the loss, determining
the gradients and updating the parameters is repeated batch by
batch until the process has passed the whole training dataset.
This completes one epoch of the training cycle. The number of
batches per epoch depends on the selected batch size.
Full training of an AI model often involves many epochs. The
loss should generally decrease over successive training epochs,
although fluctuations may occur, particularly during the initial
epochs (Fort et al., 2020).
2.2.3. Loss function monitoring and early stopping
To determine when an AI model is well trained, the loss
function should be monitored on both the training set and a
held-out validation set. When the training loss (loss function
evaluated on the training dataset) continues to decrease while the
validation loss (loss function evaluated on the validation dataset)
starts to increase, this indicates that the model is overfitting the
training data. The minimum validation loss can therefore be
used to identify an appropriate point for stopping training, often
using an early stopping procedure (Prechelt, 2012). The weights
obtained at the selected point during training are subsequently
used to calculate predictions for new, previously unseen input
data.
2.2.4. Common types of machine learning
What exactly a network learns depends on the type of learning
that is being employed. In supervised learning every input is
accompanied by a known true value, and the network learns to
produce a correct output for inputs it has not seen (Goodfellow
et al., 2016b). The loss function in this case measures the error
between the output scores and the desired scores, and learning
adjusts the weights to reduce it (LeCun et al., 2015).
In unsupervised learning only the inputs are available, and
the network learns properties of the data itself, for example to
group entries by their distinct features (Goodfellow et al., 2016b).
The loss function in unsupervised learning expresses how well
the model captures the underlying data patterns (Goodfellow et
al., 2016b).
In reinforcement learning, a neural network observes a con￾nected environment that gives an input, after which the neural
network chooses an action that changes the environment and
results in a new input for the network (Kaelbling et al., 1996). In
a guided trial and error effort the neural network can learn from
its actions and their consequences on the environment (Kaelbling
et al., 1996).
2.3. Verifying model training and predictive performance
Training diagnostics assess whether the model was trained
as intended and whether the training process shows indications
of failure. This includes checking whether the training was
completed successfully, if the loss function decreased during the
epochs in the training, and how the learned parameters behave
during the training.
Weight distribution analysis indicates how weights behave
during training. Adjusting the weights and assessing the out￾come is how a model learns, and weights represent the strength
and direction of relationships between neurons within the net￾work, e.g. a negative number meaning a negative relationship
(Goodfellow et al., 2016a). In weight distribution analysis, it is
examined how abstract parameters relate to each other during
or after training (Glorot & Bengio, 2010). These weights are
visualized for one layer or for the whole network and plotted
as a histogram (Glorot & Bengio, 2010; Li et al., 2024). The
weights are plotted on the x-axis. The resulting distributions
can be compared between training stages or between different
training runs (Glorot & Bengio, 2010; Li et al., 2024).
Most measures of classification performance derive from
the confusion matrix, which counts true positives, true nega￾tives, false positives and false negatives. Accuracy expresses
the proportion of all predictions that are correct, and precision
expresses the proportion of predicted positives that are truly
positive (Hicks et al., 2022). Accuracy can be misleading when
classes are imbalanced, because a model can achieve high ac￾curacy by predominantly predicting the majority class while
performing poorly on the minority class. Precision should be
interpreted together with recall (sensitivity), which measures
how many of the actual positive cases are detected (Hicks et al.,
2022).
After training, model performance should be validated against
known outcomes using statistical metrics. For binary classifiers,
the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve plots the true

positive rate against the false positive rate across all decision
thresholds. The area under this curve (ROC-AUC) provides a
threshold-independent measure of predictive capability: values
near 1 indicate strong performance, 0.5 reflects random classifi￾cation, and values below 0.5 indicate performance worse than
random ranking (Gneiting & Vogel, 2022).
ROC-AUC can be a useful measure, but does not directly
reflect the precision of positive predictions and can therefore
provide an incomplete picture when performance on the minority
class is of particular interest (Davis & Goadrich, 2006; Powers,
2011). For example, in a model trained to detect a rare disease
within a largely healthy population, poor detection of the minority
class may have only limited influence on the overall ROC curve
despite being practically important.
For imbalanced datasets, the Matthews Correlation Coeffi￾cient (MCC) is often more informative than the ROC-AUC. MCC
incorporates all four outcomes of the confusion matrix (Chicco
& Jurman, 2023). The metric ranges from −1, representing com￾plete disagreement between prediction and observation, to 1,
representing perfect classification; a value around 0 indicates
performance no better than random prediction. Because MCC
considers all classes simultaneously, it is regarded as a balanced
performance metric for skewed datasets. Another validation
method for imbalanced datasets is the precision-recall curve
(PRC) (Saito & Rehmsmeier, 2015).
Data leakage should always be checked to ensure honest
model performance. This can be assessed by checking whether the
training and test datasets are clearly separated, whether duplicate
or highly similar samples occur in both datasets, and whether
preprocessing steps such as normalization, feature selection, or
data augmentation were performed using the complete dataset
before splitting (Kapoor et al., 2024; Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023).
All are potential causes of data leakage.
3. Computational stack and development tools for AI models
An AI model runs on a layered stack of hardware and
software. Each layer of software depends on the layer below
it, so each needs to be present in a compatible version. In this
stack, information of the running AI model flows from top to
bottom and back. An environment is made, which is a project￾specific collection of a programming language interpreter and
installable packages. The stack is built and externally maintained,
through development tools and an environment manager that
the user operates (increasingly with generative AI as coding
assistance). Figure 1 represents this structure schematically, with
the execution path on the left and the developmental path on the
right. Each layer is discussed in turn, from the bottom up.
3.1. Computing resources
Computing resources are hardware that comprise the physical
components to perform computations. Given the computational
demands of AI, access to a capable GPU is strongly recommended.
GPUs are optimized for parallel processing, enabling efficient
handling of large, high-dimensional datasets typical in machine
learning workflows (Raina et al., 2009).
3.1.1. Local hardware
High-performance or gaming PCs are decent entry-level AI
hardware, especially when the system contains a dedicated GPU.
Memory is often a bottleneck in training complex AI models,
and ways to decrease memory consumption during training can
be found in Section 5.3 (T. Chen et al., 2016; Ojika et al., 2020;
Rajbhandari et al., 2020).
3.1.2. Cloud computing resources
An alternative to local hardware is cloud computing. Cloud
computing is the on-demand use of scalable off-site comput￾ing resources (International Organization for Standardization,
2023). Commercial cloud providers for AI applications include
Microsoft Azure, Amazon Web Services (AWS), Google Cloud
Platform (GCP) and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI). Costs
depend on factors such as computing power, user agreement dura￾tion, and the use of spot instances. Spot instances are discounted
computing capacity offered when the cloud service provider has
overcapacity (Amazon, 2026). National research and education
networks (NRENs) are government-funded academic cloud in￾frastructure, such as the Dutch IT cooperative Samenwerkende
Universitaire RekenFaciliteiten (SURF), and may offer a cost￾effective and safer alternative for eligible researchers (GÉANT,
n.d.; SURF, 2026a).
A practical concern with commercial cloud computing is
data security. Sensitive data uploaded to off-site infrastructure
may be subject to cross-border legal frameworks. For example,
U.S. legislation can compel disclosure of data held by U.S.-based
providers, even when stored in Europe (U.S. Department of
Justice, 2018). Cloud providers may also use uploaded data for
service improvement or AI training purposes, although opt-out
mechanisms are available. It is advised not to upload privacy￾sensitive information to off-site infrastructure, such as a cloud
provider, but to anonymize sensitive data or work with synthetic
data to reduce privacy risks (European Union, 2016; van Velzen
et al., 2025).
3.1.3. Hybrid computing infrastructure
A hybrid infrastructure combining local and cloud computing
may provide a practical solution for larger AI workloads. Initial
installation, troubleshooting, and exploratory runs can first be
performed locally to familiarize users with the model and identify
technical issues before migrating to cloud infrastructure. This
approach reduces unnecessary cloud GPU usage and associated
costs in the startup phase.
3.2. Operating system
The computing hardware is controlled by its operating system
(OS), which is called system software. Examples are Linux,
Windows and macOS (J. Wang et al., 2023). Compatibility
between AI models and OS environments is not guaranteed;
even minor version differences can introduce instability or errors
(J. Wang et al., 2023). Reproducing the original development
environment as closely as possible is therefore advisable. This
can be done via a local container, such as a Linux container
(Flinders & Smalley, 2025).

3.3. Runtime system
The operating system interacts with a user’s applications via
a runtime system. The runtime system is software that allows
code to execute, which is therefore programming language spe￾cific. Python, for example, is a computer language with its own
interpreters, like CPython or PyPy (Python Software Foundation,
n.d.). The interpreter’s task is to translate the programming lan￾guage like Python in order for the operating system to understand
it, perform tasks and allocate memory (Aho et al., 2006; Python
Software Foundation, n.d.). For GPU-accelerated workloads,
additional runtime components are often required. A common
example is CUDA, which provides the necessary interface be￾tween software and NVIDIA GPUs, handling memory allocation
and parallel task execution.
3.4. Libraries and packages
Above the runtime system sits a layer of libraries and packages.
This is reusable code that the application code calls upon for
tasks it does not perform itself. A library is a collection of
functions written for a general purpose, and a package is the
distributable unit in which a library is installed. In Python, pandas
provides functions for reading and manipulating tabular data
such as CSV files (McKinney, 2010), NumPy provides array
operations (Harris et al., 2020), and matplotlib renders results
as graphs (Hunter, 2007). Libraries of this kind support loading
data, transforming it, and visualizing outcomes. The application
code lists them as dependencies, and each one must be present
in the environment in a compatible version.
3.4.1. Neural network frameworks
Neural network frameworks are libraries that form the ex￾ecutional backbone of AI models. These frameworks consist
of three components: variables, functions, and parametric func￾tions (Narihira et al., 2021). Variables represent the data used
in the model and are stored in multidimensional arrays, such
as vectors or tensors (Narihira et al., 2021). Functions perform
mathematical operations on these variables, whereas parametric
functions contain trainable parameters that are adjusted during
training according to relationships identified in the training data.
The model’s weights are stored within these parametric func￾tions. The building of a model with parametric functions is more
straightforward thanks to neural network frameworks like Ten￾sorFlow (Abadi et al., 2016) and PyTorch (Paszke et al., 2019),
because they automatically track operations in the forward pass
and compute gradients in the backward pass (Ba Alawi, 2025).
3.5. User code: application scripts and notebooks
The top layer holds the code the user writes. In a single
script or notebook, the user calls on the packages from the layers
below and arranges them in the order the work requires: loading
the data, preparing it with the appropriate libraries, and passing
it to the neural network framework in small portions called
batches. The same file specifies how the model is trained, which
settings the optimization uses, and at which points the results are
checked against validation data. Once the model has produced
its predictions, supporting libraries turn these into figures and
tables the user can interpret. The user code can be executed as a
script in the used development tool.
3.6. Environment and package managers
AI models depend on a collection of software packages,
often with strict version requirements. Environment and package
managers (e.g., Conda, Mamba, Pip) facilitate the installation
and isolation of these dependencies within dedicated environ￾ments. This isolation is critical, as incompatibilities between

3.8. Generative AI as a coding aid
Programming requires translating ideas into instructions a
computer can execute, a skill that novices find difficult because
of abstract concepts and entry barriers such as unfamiliar syn￾tax, delimiters, indentation, and uninterpretable error messages
(Figueiredo & García-Peñalvo, 2018). When implementing AI
models from literature however, domain expertise remains the
primary requirement, while the coding barrier can be lowered
through generative AI (GenAI) assistance. AI-assisted coding
tools provide personalized help, on-demand feedback, and struc￾tured guidance in an educational setting (Barzanji & Loitsch,
2025), furthermore they have been shown to increase intrinsic mo￾tivation and reduce stress compared to unsupported approaches
(Fan et al., 2025).
The underlying observations translate to non-programmer
scientists. Access to AI coding support improves coding speed
and quality without impairing the ability to understand or modify
the resulting code (Kazemitabaar et al., 2023). This suggests
that AI assistance can complement the development of practical
programming skills. Across the whole programming workflow,
these tools automate repetitive tasks, generate or explain code in
natural language, and provide context-aware suggestions (e.g.,
relevant packages or functions) (Sergeyuk et al., 2025; Treude
& Gerosa, 2025). Interaction modes range from conversational
guidance to prompt-based code generation.
In practice, this support is accessed through tools such as
GitHub Copilot, integrated into development tools like Visual
Studio Code (Zhou et al., 2025), or assistants such as ChatGPT
which can act as a collaborative programming partner (Hamza
et al., 2024). These developments allow non-programmer scien￾tists to engage with AI model implementation and rely less on

4. PRAISE: the workflow for the implementation of pub￾lished AI models
The Practical, Reproducible AI-model Implementation for
Science and Education (PRAISE) workflow suggested in Figure 2
aims to help non-programmer scientists to implement existing
scientific AI models in practical case studies. The expected
output and checks the workflow should pass before continuation
to the next step are given for each step. When these checks are
not met, the workflow should be extended with fallback routes
to address these issues. This section will purely focus on the
basic workflow, and Section 5 will go into the fallback routes for
potential issues.
Complete and accurate documentation should be maintained
throughout the workflow, with each action recorded as it is
performed to support later troubleshooting and reproducibility
on other computer systems. A private Git repository is recom￾mended as an effective way to maintain this documentation. Its
version-control system records each modification chronologi￾cally, creating a traceable log of the workflow and supporting
subsequent review, troubleshooting, and reproduction.
4.1. Use case formulation
Arguably the most important step in the PRAISE workflow is
the proper formulation of a use case. A clearly defined problem is
essential for determining whether AI or ML offers an appropriate
solution, or whether conventional IT or data-management ap￾proaches would be more suitable. In short: AI/ML must address a
defined problem rather than serve as a default solution. Moreover,
this aids the selection of what kind of AI or ML is required.
Hofmann et al. proposed a guide to formulate AI use cases,
as this is not a trivial undertaking (Hofmann et al., 2020).
Here the proposed guide is followed with modifications in the
reformulation of the ‘problem and solution alignment’ and the
inclusion of regulatory compliance (Figure 3).
4.1.1. Simultaneous problem and solution discovery
Central to the modification in problem and solution alignment
from the original guide is that problem and solution discovery
were performed consecutively, while in our view problem and
solution discoveries can be performed simultaneously. This
process should be repeated until the problem and the proposed
solution are aligned. An initially vague problem can guide
a search for potential AI solutions; studying these solutions
clarifies which problems can be addressed, thereby enabling a
more precise formulation of the original problem. This refined
formulated problem is the starting point of the next iteration.
The process naturally terminates when formulated problem and
proposed AI solution become aligned.
When selecting potentially usable candidate models, the
following should be considered:
• Recency: AI research advances fast, so a model of several
years old is likely deprecated, although a more mature
model often carries fewer bugs and more solved issues.

• Developer support and community involvement: an active
repository indicates that the model is kept working as
dependencies and frameworks evolve, and that installation
problems have been previously discussed.
• Available validation options: models that ship with ways
to assess accuracy, robustness or feature attribution are
preferred, as adding these afterwards requires program￾ming expertise (Section 4.5). Acceptance values for the
chosen validation metric (Section 2.3) should be set here
to determine when model performance is acceptable.
4.1.2. Risk assessment and compliance
In addition, here the use case formulation guide by Hofmann
et al. (2020) is extended by incorporating a risk assessment and
compliance check, particularly in line with the EU AI Act (Euro￾pean Commission, 2023). This step is essential to incorporate
data security and regulatory constraints from the start. This may
include compliance with privacy regulations such as the General
Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) (European Union, 2016),
intellectual property (IP) rights, and non-disclosure agreements
(NDA). Information protection is strengthened by aligning local
data handling with established standards such as ISO/IEC 27001
(information security management system) (International Organi￾zation for Standardization, 2022a) and ISO/IEC 27701 (privacy
information management system) (International Organization for
Standardization, 2025). The safety of local large language model
(LLM) use should be assessed against the Open Worldwide
Application Security Project (OWASP) guidelines for LLM risks.
These guidelines include (but are not limited to) prompt injection,
sensitive information disclosure, and insecure output handling
(OWASP, 2024). The use case reporting framework proposed
by Hupont et al. (2024) can be used to systematically collect
contextual information, in order to estimate the associated risk
level of AI models. Particular caution is required when models
process personal data or are applied in sensitive domains such as
eligibility decisions, law, migration, or elections (Hupont et al.,
2024; van Velzen et al., 2025).

4.1.3. Implementation and documentation
Hofmann et al. (2020) describe implementation in terms of
development responsibilities, system integration, data handling,
and ethical considerations. The workflow presented here adds
practical steps for implementing the model itself: dataset prepa￾ration, infrastructure selection, model training, and validation
(Sections 4.2 to 4.5), with troubleshooting in Section 5. The
output of use case formulation is a document that reports the
defined problem, the intended use (users, input and output), the
candidate model, the possible risk level, and the acceptance
criteria for model performance. These criteria are set before
training. They state which performance metrics (Section 2.3)
will be used to validate the model, for example on predictive
performance, and which threshold values the model must reach
to pass validation.
4.2. Dataset preparation
Data is the foundation of a predictive AI model and often the
limiting factor in machine learning (Roh et al., 2021). Hence, the
essential step of dataset preparation should be performed with
care.
To be able to reproduce a model from literature, the dataset
should be provided by the author of that model. Datasets should
be Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable (FAIR)
(Wilkinson et al., 2016). Often the used datasets are included
with the source code at a repository maintained by the author.
Once access to the dataset has been obtained, its validity
for the use case should be evaluated according to the criteria
set out in Section 2.1 such as: dataset size, training set gaps,
biases or the absence of negative datapoints. Assessment should
address how well the dataset represents expected situations in
the intended use case and how its inevitable imperfections may
affect model-training performance.
Depending on the outcome of these analyses, the dataset
needs to be supplemented with additional data, to improve the
coverage of the dataset. The required additional data may be
gathered from public repositories, automated extraction of online
sources (data scraping) or newly generated experimental or
synthetic data. A manual literature search to build a dataset is
time consuming. Data scraping models can speed up this process,
although automatic data extraction from the internet must be
performed responsibly (Ayuso et al., 2024; Kahlon & Singh,
2026).
Finally, as emphasized in Section 2.1 the (improved) dataset
should be divided into subsets for testing, training and validation,
while avoiding data leakage as much as possible. The split can
be checked on four points, together forming the leakage protocol,
condensed from the leakage items proposed by Kapoor et al.
(Kapoor et al., 2024; Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023).
• Split order. The dataset is split into training, validation and
test sets before any preprocessing, normalization, feature
selection or augmentation. Preprocessing parameters are
derived from the training set only.
• Overlap. After splitting, the subsets share no identical
records. Where records are related or highly similar, they
are assigned to the same subset according to a defined
similarity criterion. The similarity criterion and strictness
of the split are reported (Cui et al., 2025; Elmi et al., 2026;
Kroll et al., 2024).
• Use of the subsets. The validation set is used for early
stopping and hyperparameter choices only. The test set is
used once, for the final metrics in Section 4.5. Any part
of the training pipeline that reads the validation or test
set for another purpose fails this check (Goodfellow et al.,
2016b).
• Reproducibility. The split is generated by a script with
a fixed random seed (Wegmeth et al., 2023). Both are
documented so the split can be regenerated.
The output of the dataset preparation is a data manifest,
containing information of the sources of the data, record count
per subset, and the script and seed for data splitting.
4.3. Computing infrastructure selection
The next step is to select the computing resources needed to
run an AI model from literature. This involves choices regarding
the properties of the computing resources, which may be local,
cloud or hybrid (see Section 3.1). The selection of the compu￾tational resources is a delicate step in which prerequisites of
the AI model and computing resources need to be balanced: if
the computing facilities are too limited, the model might simply
not run or take an inordinate timespan to execute, while if the
resources are too large managing them becomes a daunting task
and will possibly quickly exceed the available budget. The check
for this step is to ensure the infrastructure meets the requirements.
The output is a chosen infrastructure as detailed in Section 3.
4.3.1. Constraints on computational infrastructure
The computing memory required to train and run AI models is
often the hardware bottleneck. Memory requirements of scientific
AI inferences are notoriously difficult to estimate and compare
since they are dependent on multiple factors like parameter
size, how intermediate embeddings are stored, and how detailed
the input is (Tian et al., 2025). Even when considering these
complications, the disparity in memory requirements between AI
models is striking (Tian et al., 2025). In an AI model replication
study, seven of the 30 models were not replicated because
the specific hardware required was practically unobtainable
(Gundersen et al., 2025).
The specifications of the computer infrastructure used by the
model developer could be used as a guideline for a required com￾puting memory. However, using a trained model often requires
much less memory than the training procedure itself (Sohoni et
al., 2019). Thus, if retraining an AI model is not part of the AI use
case, and the trained model is provided, the user would not need
to match the developer’s computational setup. If retraining is
part of the AI use case, matching the computing infrastructure is
fair for an honest comparison and full reproducibility. When this
is not possible due to the earlier described balance in resources,
the training and computing time will be longer. If this is not
acceptable, the model can be downsized or the step to cloud
computing can be taken (Section 5.3).

4.3.2. Local hardware computing
Dedicated local hardware for computing is ideal if the avail￾able or intended hardware is close to the specifications of the
infrastructure used by the author of the used AI model. Local
computation resources have the advantage that training, running
and adjusting AI models can be done without additional token
costs and access to the facilities is unconstrained. However, the
investment might be substantial if the hardware needs to be
purchased for this purpose only.
4.3.3. Cloud and hybrid computing
Cloud computing is an option when local hardware cannot
support the model, even after optimization. Compared to on-site
infrastructure, cloud computing has relatively low startup costs
and can be deployed quickly, but comes with higher costs for
prolonged GPU usage (Armbrust et al., 2010). For small-scale or
exploratory work, local infrastructure remains preferable when
suitable hardware is available. It is also worthwhile to consider
a hybrid approach where initial setup optimizations are done
with the local hardware so that heavy computational jobs can be
efficiently performed in the cloud infrastructure.
4.3.4. OS selection
The basic rule of thumb for the choice of the operating
system is to use the OS which was employed by the authors
of the published AI model. Converting routines used on one
platform to the next is in principle possible but is likely to fail
given the complexity of using or training models and the many
dependencies of the computational environment. If the required
OS is not available, containers or full virtualizations can be
used, though this often reduces performance. For local hardware,
setting up a dual boot environment with different operating
systems alongside each other might be an option.
4.4. Running an AI model: from installation to computing
Running a published AI model requires its code and a match￾ing software environment on the local machine. Training the
model additionally requires the training dataset and more com￾putational resources than inference with pretrained models. In￾ference requires the pretrained model with its weights.
4.4.1. Published AI model repository
Requirements depend primarily on the architecture and im￾plementation of the published AI model. Datasets, code and
documentation of a published AI model are typically available
on GitHub. The repository, i.e. all folders and files, can be cloned
to the local machine through the git extension in a development
tool like Visual Studio Code or JupyterLab.
4.4.2. Development tools
The choice of development tools is highly personal. Jupyter￾Lab is web-based and designed to be user-friendly, Visual Studio
Code needs to be installed and is more complex due to its cus￾tomizability (Kluyver et al., 2016). Beginners in programming
are advised to try multiple tools to determine the ones they may
prefer. Open-source availability does not imply security; the code,
dependencies, and documentation should therefore be reviewed
critically.
4.4.3. Computational environment
Before executing the code, an environment should be con￾figured (Section 3.6). The documentation accompanying the
AI model specifies the required programming language version
and packages. In the development tool, the appropriate envi￾ronment can be selected and the required packages installed
via a command in the integrated terminal. A check should be
performed for all required files, like datasets or trained model
weights. These often need to be downloaded separately. File
paths in the code should be checked and possibly adjusted to fit
the repository structure. GenAI tools like Codex, Claude Code or
GitHub Copilot can be used as personal programming assistants
(Section 3.8).
4.4.4. Running the AI model
With the repository, environment and data locally available,
the training or inference of the AI model can start. Ensure that
the computational work is done by the GPU. Checkpoints can
be saved in case the training stopped (TensorFlow, 2024). The
output can be checked against expectation at each checkpoint.
When the execution has been completed within an acceptable
time, one can continue with validating the model (Section 4.5). If
the hardware appears to be insufficient for model execution, the
model might be downsized, or hardware expanded (Section 5.2
and 5.3).
Once training is complete, the model is saved in framework￾specific formats, for PyTorch or TensorFlow. Pretrained models
are shared in these same formats. Hugging Face developed
Safetensors, which saves metadata in JSON format and is uni￾versally usable for different programming languages. Local
inference might then be possible, since these calculations require
less computing power than training. Both locally trained and
downloaded pretrained models can be loaded for inference on
the local machine with dedicated code.
4.4.5. Basic after-training checks
Whether a training run completed as intended can be judged
from the computing time, the presence of the expected output
files, and the values these files contain. A computing time far
below the expected value or missing output files indicates that the
training did not complete. Output files in which every prediction
has the same value, for example 0.5 for a binary classifier, indicate
that the model did not learn during training. Validation is only
useful once none of these signs are observed.
4.5. Model validation
The basic question of the validation step is whether the
results of the AI model warrant deployment. Reliable AI model
predictions depend on the quality of the training data and on what
the model has actually learned from it (Hartung & Kleinstreuer,
2025). Weight distributions (Section 2.3) can be compared
between training runs to indicate whether each run produced a
model of comparable quality, and a tool such as WeightWatcher
automates this comparison by analyzing the weight matrix of
every layer and reporting a quality value per layer, which helps to
identify layers that remained undertrained or became overtrained
(Martin et al., 2021).

The performance indicators introduced in Section 2.3 can
be calculated on the validation set during training and on the
test set once training is finished. Prediction scores and true
labels are the input for ROC-AUC calculations. MCC oper￾ates on discrete class labels and therefore requires a thresh￾old, commonly 0.5. Standard options (such as roc_auc_score,
average_precision_score, matthews_corrcoef) are avail￾able in scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011). Repeating the cal￾culation over several random seeds or data splits indicates how
stable the obtained values are. Whether the obtained predictive
performance metrics are acceptable depends on the use case.
Calculating the same indicators for the part of the test set that
lies furthest from the training data shows how well the model
covers gaps in the dataset and how it behaves on unseen input.
Performance indicators do not need to match those reported
for the original model exactly. Variations in outcomes might
be caused by differences in the random seed used for weight
initialization, the order in which data are processed in parallel,
or the GPU libraries (cuDNN and CUDA) (Pham et al., 2021).
Training runs across three datasets and six AI networks, with
a fixed random seed, resulted in accuracy variations of up to
2.9% (Pham et al., 2021). Whether the validation was adequate
is recorded in the use case formulation document (Section 4.1).
If it does not meet these requirements, Section 5.4 helps locate
the cause.
4.6. Deployment
When validation meets the acceptance criteria set in the use
case document (Section 4.1.3), the model can be deployed in
the use case. Predictions for new input data can be made, in
the context described in the use case document: who uses the
model, which input they provide, and which output they need.
A short test with new, representative input confirms that the
model is ready for this use. The model should accept the input
format of the users, return output they can interpret, and run
on the hardware available to them. A low-code web-based user
interface gives users without programming experience access to
the trained model (Kroll et al., 2023).
At this point the logbook with documentation of the im￾plementation process can be turned into a wiki that others can
follow. An LLM such as Claude or Copilot is a useful drafting aid
here, if the result is checked against the original implementation
to avoid propagating errors. The outputs presented in Figure 2
collectively form the reproducibility package: use case document,
data manifest and split script, hardware and OS record, environ￾ment file, run commands, logs, model files and metrics. This
package meets the bronze standard of Heil et al. (2021). Before
publishing, the package can be checked against a reproducibility
checklist such as REFORMS (Kapoor et al., 2024; Kwak & Kim,
2025). Code, data and documentation should be published in a
public GitHub repository linked from the paper. Large datasets
belong on Zenodo or DataverseNL, under their own DOI cited
in the paper.
5. Fallback troubleshooting by workflow iteration
The PRAISE workflow as presented in the previous section
indicates the ideal situation, in which all steps can be executed
without any difficulties. In practice, this will rarely be the case,
thus the workflow needs to be adjusted accordingly at various
stages when issues arise. The workflow diagram is extended with
these troubleshooting routes (Figure 4).
5.1. Analyzing dataset mismatch
Dataset preparation fails when the use case and the available
data do not match, because the dataset should cover the intended
applicability domain of the model. The data needed for the
use case might not be available or not of appropriate quality
even after extension according to the procedures described in
Section 4.2. The use case should then be reformulated to fit the
available data while keeping the project relevant.
If changing the use case is undesirable and the mismatch
comprises only minor gaps, dataset preparation and the remaining
workflow can proceed, with the gaps documented as limitations
on model validity. These limitations should be revisited during
validation (Section 4.5), where performance can be assessed
separately across poorly represented regions of the applicability
domain.
5.2. Assessing computational infrastructure
Computational bottlenecks can sometimes be foreseen when
the computing infrastructure is selected, but they can also become
apparent during or after installation and test runs. In that case,
the user code is revised first (Section 5.3). The infrastructure
is reassessed when code changes are insufficient. Infrastructure
here covers all layers of Figure 1 below the user code: libraries,
runtime, operating system and computing hardware.
It is essential to first analyze what the main underlying
reasons for the issues could be. Common issues are that the
system runs out of memory, runs out of storage capacity, takes
too long to complete or delivers unexpected outputs. Possible
remedies essentially fall into a few categories:
• adjust the computational infrastructure
• adjust the datasets
• reformulate the use case
Often a combination of these remedies is needed to solve com￾putational infrastructure issues in the workflow.
5.2.1. Reselect infrastructure
The limits on computational performance can sometimes be
raised with minor adjustments, such as extending the memory,
acquiring more compute credits, or installing a different package
in the environment. However, often this will entail a more
major upgrade of the on-site hardware or move to cloud/hybrid
computing (Section 3.1). To decide which adjustment is needed,
the issues must be analyzed in detail to define the additional
infrastructure requirements. A conclusion can be that the issues
are not practically or financially solvable. In that case, the
limitation must be accepted, and the solution shifts to reducing
the dataset size or reformulating the use case (Section 4.1 and
Section 4.2).

Another option could be to modify the questions of the use

5.3. Revising user code
Problems during installation and computing, such as a run that
crashes, runs out of memory, takes too long or gives unexpected
outputs, can often be solved by revising the user code and
recomputing. When the cause lies in computing infrastructure, for
example a failed installation or package conflict, the infrastructure
is reassessed (Section 5.2).
5.3.1. Model downsizing
To make a model fit the computational requirements within
the existing infrastructure, downsizing of the model can be
considered. One downsizing method is reducing the batch size,
which is configurable in frameworks like PyTorch or TensorFlow,
though smaller batches produce noisier gradients (Keskar et al.,
2017). Other common approaches include gradient checkpoint￾ing, precision scaling, and gradient accumulation.
5.3.2. Gradient checkpointing
Another way to reduce infrastructure requirements is gradient
checkpointing. This adjustment reduces memory usage by stor￾ing less information during the forward pass and recalculating
intermediate values when needed (T. Chen et al., 2016). This
adjustment reduced the memory consumption of a thousand-layer
AI model sixfold with only a 30% increase in computation time.
5.3.3. Precision scaling
Further lowering of memory requirements can be achieved
by precision scaling. This method reduces the numerical preci￾sion used within the model, for example from 32-bit to 16-bit
values, thus resulting in lower memory requirements while often
maintaining comparable model performance (Dettmers et al.,
2022).
5.3.4. Gradient accumulation
Gradient accumulation spreads gradient updates across multi￾ple smaller batches before adjusting model parameters, allowing
larger models to be trained on limited hardware (Rajbhandari
et al., 2020). Regardless of the approach, the resulting model
performance should be validated against outcomes reported in
the literature to confirm that downsizing did not negatively affect
results. If downsizing is insufficient, the use of pretrained models
may be a more suitable alternative.

5.3.5. Alternative types of machine learning
As reviewed in Section 2.2, there are various types of machine
learning with corresponding loss functions to be minimized. It is
worth considering whether there are modifications in the applied
training procedure to alleviate the computational bottlenecks (as
different implementations might have impact on the required
computational power, memory, etc.). If code changes do not
resolve the issue, the next step is to reassess the infrastructure or
to adjust the dataset or use case.
5.4. Diagnosing validation issues
If the validation of the trained model does not yield acceptable
results, model diagnostics should be performed on the dataset,
the scripts and/or notebooks. An unmodified reproduction of
the published model is expected to reach performance metrics
comparable to those reported by the authors, but variations in
validation outcomes might occur (Section 4.5).
5.4.1. Dataset Composition and Validation Performance
Modifications to the dataset influence the validation outcome.
In supervised learning, a different data split (Section 2.1) changes
which samples the model is optimized on and which are held
out, so the reported metrics shift as well (Cawley & Talbot,
2010). Extending the original dataset with additional records can
improve predictive performance and can widen the coverage of
the model (Rajput et al., 2023; Z. Wang et al., 2026).
5.4.2. Permutation Testing for Data Leakage
When metrics are higher than expected, a permutation test
can be performed. Here, the training labels are shuffled at ran￾dom, and the model is retrained to check whether predictive
performance is attributable to relationships in the original data.
In the permutation test, performance should approach the random
baseline (ROC-AUC ≈ 0.5 and MCC ≈ 0). Substantially higher
performance indicates that information remains available to the
model despite removal of the original label-feature relationship
and may point to data leakage or an error in the training or
evaluation procedure (Ojala & Garriga, 2010).
5.4.3. Diagnosing Deviations from the Published Model
Finally, the training and evaluation scripts should be com￾pared with the published implementation to identify unintended
changes in data handling, preprocessing, model configuration,
optimization, or evaluation. Changes to how data is preprocessed
or separated before a training can introduce data leakage and
result in misleading performance estimates (Kapoor et al., 2024).
Other differences, such as the learning rate, batch size, number
of training epochs, or model parameters, can alter the training
outcome even when the dataset is unchanged. The diagnostics
should therefore establish whether obtained unexpected valida￾tion performances originate from issues in the validation protocol,
differences in the dataset or issues in the training process. If
none of these solve the validation issues, the use case should be
reformulated.
6. Case study of workflow application in research and edu￾cation
The preceding sections present the PRAISE workflow as
a sequence of generic steps. This section illustrates how these
steps were executed in a worked example. The sections follow
the actual course of the project: a first pass through the steps
of Figure 4, then four iterations, each triggered by an issue that
required to return to a previous step (Figure 5). These loops are
an integral part of the PRAISE workflow.
The case concerns the prediction of enzyme-substrate inter￾actions in a chemical recycling project. Three third-year BSc
chemistry students and their tutor selected a published deep
learning model, reproduced it, validated its performance and
extended it to their own dataset within four months, using GenAI
programming assistants. The tutor was a chemistry teacher and re￾searcher, who helped formulate the use case and select computing
infrastructure. None of them had a background in programming
or data science.
6.1. First pass: reproducing ProSmith
The first pass started with the formulation of the use case (Sec￾tion 4.1). The work is part of C-Recycle, an EU Just Transition
Fund project on chemical recycling, where waste streams such
as plastic pyrolysis oil are upcycled into high-value chemicals
that serve as feedstock for industry (Stimulus, 2024). Oxidative
enzymes can functionalize these streams, but finding suitable
enzyme–substrate pairs experimentally is laborious, and public
databases only cover characterized pairs. The aim was therefore
to reproduce an existing prediction model and deploy it in this
research to match enzymes to substrates, find candidate enzymes
and prioritize mutants for testing. The model would be consid￾ered ready for deployment when it reproduced the performance
reported by its authors on the held-out test set and could predict
interactions for new enzyme-substrate pairs.
With the use case defined, a limited literature search at the
start of the project in 2024 identified the Enzyme Substrate
Predictor (ESP) and subsequently ProSmith as suitable models
(Kroll et al., 2023; Kroll et al., 2024). Further models appeared
during implementation and were also considered (Campbell,
2024; Cui et al., 2025; Du et al., 2025; Elmi et al., 2025, 2026).
Publication in this field is rapid enough that a selected model can
be superseded before its implementation is completed, which
makes model choice a recurring decision throughout the project,
and possibly an iteration loop back to this step.
The risk assessment and compliance check of Section 4.1
was carried out before implementation. No personal data were
processed, and the application falls outside the sensitive domains
identified there, placing the use case at a minimal risk level under
the EU AI Act.
The project started with the ProSmith ESP dataset (Kroll et
al., 2024). ProSmith was trained on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU
with 40 GB memory, and training on a 24 GB NVIDIA RTX
6000 yielded similar performance at the cost of a longer run
time. A 24 GB GPU would therefore suffice. This local training
was done in the HR Datalab EAS, an in-house facility with high￾performance workstations shared under clear usage guidelines.
The workstations contained an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090

models, trained on the training set only, which is validated, tested,
and saved as one object. The cost of this choice is that the final
models were trained on fewer records.
Two other adaptations were made. The trained gradient
boosting models were discarded after training, and predictions
were not automatically linked back to their original enzyme￾substrate records. The pipeline was restructured to train the
model on the training set only, to save models and ensemble
weights automatically, and to map predictions to the original
records (step 4 of Figure 5).
6.6. Comparison of performance metrics across training ses￾sions
For each ProSmith training session, accuracy, precision,
ROC-AUC and MCC (Section 2.3) were calculated on the test
set (Table 1). All trainings were executed once, so the values
are indicative only. ProSmith remains predictive for the supple￾mented datasets, as the positive MCC scores show. The lower
MCC after iteration 4 was suspected to be a result of data
leakage, but was eventually deemed consistent with the smaller
training set of the restructured pipeline and falls within the run￾to-run variation reported for deep learning pipelines (Pham et al.,
2021). The restructured pipeline met the criteria of the use case
(Section 6.1). Validation was therefore considered successful,
deployment could commence.
6.7. Deployment
The retrained ProSmith model was deployed as a prediction
script on SURF Research Cloud. The script loaded the saved
model and ensemble weights, predicted interactions for new
enzyme-substrate pairs, and mapped each prediction to its input
record. The trained model could be used to screen enzymes for
potential interactions with a substrate of interest, for example in
functionalization reactions or in the degradation of waste streams.
A second application was the prediction of how point mutations
affected enzyme-substrate interaction. Both applications still
require future experimental validation before the predictions
can guide enzyme selection. The data, models and code of all
iterations are available via the authors’ GitHub page.
6.8. Case study outcome
Three BSc chemistry students and their tutor, all without
programming experience, were able to reproduce and extend the
predictive model ProSmith within four months using the PRAISE
workflow. The model and computing infrastructure were selected
by the institution, and the students performed the installation,
data management, model training and validation independently.
They adapted the pipeline and applied it to additional datasets
(Table 1). The implementation required four iterations. Each
started where the expected result was not obtained and the
students returned to an earlier step (Figure 5). The workflow
allowed the students to understand each step and how to solve
problems they encountered. A separate paper on the educational
implementation of the PRAISE workflow into the BSc chemistry
program is in preparation.
7. Discussion
We developed the structured, reproducible workflow PRAISE,
which guides non-programmer scientists through implementing
an AI model published in the literature. The workflow divides
the process into explicit, verifiable steps and includes fallback
loops for common issues.
By explicitly incorporating dataset and model validation, the
PRAISE workflow positions reproducibility as a prerequisite
in the implementation of AI models. The PRAISE workflow
covers performance metrics that indicate whether predictions
are accurate. The scientific value of the resulting predictions
is a separate question that the user should assess themselves.
Feature analysis can indicate which characteristics in the data
are responsible for certain predictions (Li et al., 2020). LIME
estimates feature importance for individual predictions by gener￾ating synthetic data around a single datapoint (Linardatos et al.,
2021; Ribeiro et al., 2016). SHAP produces local explanations
and global importance scores across the model (Linardatos et
al., 2021; Lundberg & Lee, 2017). In models of the complexity
of ProSmith, these methods are difficult to apply, because the
model inputs are learned vector representations produced by
several preprocessing steps, and individual dimensions carry no
direct chemical interpretation. Feature analysis is therefore not
included as a validation step in the proposed workflow.
Several other disclaimers for adopting the PRAISE workflow
exist. One is that basic computer and data skills remain neces￾sary, such as installing a program, understanding documentation
14

=== PDF PAGE 15 ===
and managing large datasets. In addition, the availability of the
published model and its dataset, and the quality of the documen￾tation, determine whether the workflow can be started at all. This
becomes clear in the first step of the PRAISE workflow, where
the use case is matched with potential AI solutions. Even with
good documentation, specialized AI models may still require
more advanced expertise than the workflow and generative AI
coding helpers can give. If large AI models require more ex￾tensive computing power than the institution can provide, these
models stay out of reach for users as well. The most restrictive
limitation is the absence of data covering the research question
of the use case, which results in unreliable predictions regardless
of the quality of the model.
In conclusion, implementing an AI model from literature is
a skill that can be learned. With the basic techniques described
here and an understanding of the PRAISE workflow steps and
their iterations, non-programmer scientists can reproduce pub￾lished models and implement them in their own practice. A
non-programmer scientist who adopts the PRAISE workflow
becomes an evaluator and improver of AI models.
