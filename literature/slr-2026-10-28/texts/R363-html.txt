Across governments, intergovernmental bodies, research institutes, and firms, guideline documents repeatedly endorse fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, and human dignity. Yet convergence on labels is often mistaken for convergence on normative content. This paper argues that much of the current discourse remains normatively influential while being insufficiently action-guiding. It develops two claims. First, normative concepts are genuinely useful only when they are determinate enough to support critique: they must specify what would count as satisfying them and what would count as failing them, and they must confront trade-offs rather than implying a moral free lunch in which all values can be maximized simultaneously. Normative claims about sociotechnical systems implicitly rely on assumptions about feasibility, institutional incentives, measurement, and human response. When these background assumptions remain unspecified or idealized, ethical guidance becomes aspirational and can even be normatively irresponsible. The paper therefore articulates minimal conditions for AI-ethical discourse that is criticizable and responsibly connected to implementation, without reducing ethics to technocracy or compliance checklists. Computer Ethics Information Ethics Normative Ethics Philosophy of Artificial Intelligence Meta-Ethics Ethics of Technology Responsible Innovation and Public Engagement in Science 1 Introduction Over the last decade, AI ethics has become a thriving industry of principles, frameworks, and policy declarations. Governments publish national strategies, intergovernmental bodies issue recommendations, research institutes release checklists, and firms announce corporate “principles” intended to reassure users and regulators alike. This proliferation is not speculative. Surveys of the field repeatedly find hundreds of guidelines and recommendations that converge on a familiar repertoire of values—fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, human dignity, non-discrimination—while diverging in interpretation, scope, and practical ambition [ 4 , 6 , 14 ]. The result is a peculiar kind of moral consensus: everyone agrees on the labels, far fewer agree on what those labels commit us to in contested cases, and fewer still can say how the commitments should constrain design choices and institutional practice. The ambition of this paper is not to add yet another list of desiderata to this already crowded landscape. Instead, it asks a prior question: Under what conditions can normative ethical principles in AI contexts be (i) conceptually determinate enough to be explicated, criticized, and compared, and (ii) practically connected enough to implementation to be more than aspirational talk? These conditions matter because AI ethics is increasingly used as a substitute for precisely the work that ethics, if it is to earn its keep, ought to perform: Enabling disagreement to be articulated, tested, and adjudicated under institutional, informational, and feasibility constraints. When ethical discourse degenerates into slogans, it becomes not only difficult to implement ethics, but even to argue about it in a way that can plausibly change anyone’s mind. A striking feature of the guideline ecosystem is how readily it produces the impression that certain properties are ethical per se. Documents tell us that AI should be “trustworthy,” “human-centered,” “fair,” or “responsible,” as if the moral work were largely completed once the right adjective is attached to the technology. The objection is not that high-level principles are illegitimate because they are high-level. General principles may serve important orienting, agenda-setting, or coalition-building functions. The difficulty arises when convergence at the level of abstract principles is treated as if it already constituted substantive ethical guidance or evidence of responsible practice. Principles alone are not self-applying. They do not tell us what to do when values conflict, when information is missing, when incentives are misaligned, or when institutional constraints make some options infeasible. Absent further conceptual and institutional machinery, they are often too indeterminate to guide action or to anchor criticism. This is now a familiar diagnosis in the critical literature on AI ethics: high-level principles are cheap, and the hard work begins precisely where they end (cf. [ 9 , 24 ]). The most prominent global instruments illustrate the basic pattern. UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence is an ambitious attempt to articulate a wide set of values and policy objectives at global scale [ 36 ]. The OECD’s Recommendation of the Council on Artificial Intelligence similarly offers principles meant to be portable across jurisdictions and compatible with innovation and economic growth [ 27 ]. The European Commission’s High-Level Expert Group has provided widely cited Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, framing a “trustworthy AI” agenda around lawful, ethical, and robust systems [ 5 ]. These texts are influential for good reasons: they are politically salient, rhetorically powerful, and institutionally convenient. Yet their very success relies on a kind of principled generality that can also undermine critical traction. If nearly any reasonable stakeholder can endorse a principle at the level of abstraction at which it is stated, then endorsement alone tells us little about what should be done when the principle bites—when it forbids something, requires a sacrifice, or demands a concrete reallocation of risk, cost, or control. This is not merely a philosophical complaint about vagueness. It has consequences for governance. If a concept is too elastic to specify what would count as a failure, then it becomes difficult to audit, difficult to contest, and easy to invoke as a reputational shield. Critics have warned that ethics discourse can become “toothless” when it functions primarily as soft assurance—an alternative to binding requirements and enforceable accountability [ 30 ]. Others worry that the very pluralism of principles documents enables a form of “ethics washing,” in which organizations publicly signal virtue while deferring the uncomfortable questions about trade-offs, enforcement, and responsibility [ 9 , 24 , 37 ]. This paper advances two arguments, which together diagnose two forms of underdetermination in AI ethics: one concerning conflict and trade-offs, the other concerning implementation. First, normative ethical concepts are genuinely useful only when they are conceptually determinate enough to support critique. Concepts that reduce to tautology (“ethical AI is AI that is ethically aligned”) or that function as unanalyzed applause lights (“trustworthy,” “responsible”) cannot play the role they are assigned in AI governance. A term that does not delimit its own criteria of fulfillment—what counts as satisfying it, what counts as violating it—cannot do the work of guiding design or underwriting accountability. A central diagnostic here is the handling of trade-offs. Ethical discourse becomes informative when it makes conflicts visible and specifies how commitments constrain choices under scarcity and disagreement. When documents imply that everything desirable can be had at once—fairness and privacy and maximal transparency and maximal security, all without cost—they invite a kind of moral free lunch that no serious ethics should promise. Second, even conceptually clear normativity is incomplete if it treats implementation as an afterthought. Normative ethics that aims to govern sociotechnical systems cannot responsibly ignore the empirical realities that shape how values are realized: institutional incentives, organizational capacity, data practices, measurement error, distributional consequences, and predictable failure modes. Translating “what we ought to value” into “what we ought to do” requires attention to mechanisms and effects—precisely the domain in which empirical ethics and related practice-oriented work can inform and discipline normative claims [ 26 ]. The claim is not that facts determine values, but that value-claims about what should be built or deployed implicitly commit their authors to assumptions about feasibility and effects. If those assumptions are left unspecified, ethics becomes merely aspirational. The goal, then, is to clarify what it would mean to move beyond buzzwords without offering yet another buzzword in their place. The paper does not propose a new grand theory of justice, dignity, or rights. Nor does it pretend to resolve deep normative disagreement by methodological fiat. Rather, it aims to articulate conditions under which normative discourse in AI can be both criticizable and action-guiding: clear enough to be contested, and grounded enough to be responsibly connected to implementation. In what follows, I first diagnose the conceptual failure modes of underdetermined ethical concepts and show why trade-offs are not an embarrassment to be suppressed but a test of whether ethics is doing any work at all. I then argue that ethical guidance for AI must be coupled to an account of implementation and feedback, drawing on the emerging literature that tries to translate principles into practices without blurring the line between normativity and technocracy. 2 Normativity without criteria The rapid proliferation of AI ethics guidelines has been accompanied by a striking convergence at the level of values and principles. Fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, and human dignity recur with remarkable consistency across institutional, national, and corporate documents [ 4 , 14 ]. This convergence is often taken as evidence of ethical progress: if diverse actors can agree on core values, then the moral foundations of AI governance might appear secure. Yet convergence at the level of labels should not be mistaken for convergence at the level of normative content. Agreement on words can coexist with deep disagreement—or indeterminacy—about what those words require, forbid, or justify in concrete cases. This section develops a diagnostic claim: normative ethical concepts are only genuinely useful if they are sufficiently determinate to enable criticism, comparison, and revision. Concepts that remain elastic, tautological, or insulated from counterexamples may retain rhetorical appeal, but they fail in their core ethical function. The problem is not that high-level principles are false or misguided,rather, it is that, absent further specification, they often lack the internal structure required to guide judgment under conditions of conflict, scarcity, and uncertainty. Ethical discourse becomes informative precisely when it exposes tensions and trade-offs, not when it obscures them behind an appearance of harmony. Normative concepts differ from purely descriptive ones in that they purport to guide action and evaluation. To say that a system is fair, responsible, or trustworthy is not merely to describe it, but to endorse it—or to signal that it satisfies standards that others ought to accept. For such concepts to play this role, however, it must be possible to say when they apply or are fulfilled and when they are not. At a minimum, a normative concept must be capable of being met—and of being violated. If no conceivable state of affairs would count against its application, then the concept collapses into vacuity. This requirement is modest but non-negotiable. Ethical concepts need not be perfectly precise, nor must they resolve all borderline cases. But they must delimit a space of admissible and inadmissible uses. Otherwise, ethical language ceases to function as a tool for criticism and becomes instead a form of moral applause. Calling a system “ethical” or “trustworthy” without specifying the conditions under which that description would be withdrawn amounts to little more than a restatement of approval. A recurring concern in the literature on AI ethics is that many principles function in precisely this way. Mittelstadt [ 24 ] argues that principles alone cannot guarantee ethical AI because they remain underdetermined with respect to implementation and conflict. This paper treats these as two distinct forms of underdetermination: underdetermination with respect to conflict, and underdetermination with respect to implementation. Hagendorff [ 9 ], in a systematic evaluation of AI ethics guidelines, shows that most documents enumerate desirable properties without providing criteria for resolving tensions between them or for assessing compliance in contested cases. The result is not merely vagueness, but a deeper problem of non-falsifiability in a broad normative sense: there is no clear account of what would count as a failure of fairness, responsibility, or trustworthiness, as opposed to a different interpretation of those same values. This is not an abstract philosophical worry detached from practice. Normative concepts that lack determinate criteria of satisfaction and failure cannot structure disagreement. They invite consensus at the level of abstraction precisely because they postpone, rather than resolve, the hard questions. As a result, ethical debate risks becoming performative: actors affirm shared values while continuing to disagree, silently and unproductively, about what those values require. One reason underdetermined concepts persist is that they support what might be called the illusion of being ethical per se. Certain design features or governance mechanisms are presented as inherently ethical, rather than as ethical relative to particular values, priorities, and background assumptions. Transparency, for example, is widely endorsed as an ethical requirement. Yet transparency can promote accountability while undermining privacy, it can enable scrutiny while increasing opportunities for manipulation; and it can impose cognitive burdens that disproportionately affect some users over others. Without an account of which of these considerations takes precedence, transparency remains an ethical slogan rather than a determinate standard. Hate speech is a clean case of the problem. International AI-ethics instruments routinely name hate speech as a salient risk, yet the term functions less as a criterion than as a placeholder for unresolved boundary disputes: definition, scope, evidential thresholds, and the permissible trade-offs with freedom of expression, privacy, and due process (cf. [ 12 , 28 ]). In adjacent human-rights governance, attempts to make hate speech action-guiding typically proceed by specifying legal tests and contextual thresholds rather than relying on the label alone. The contrast illustrates the general point: without criteria, normative terms can mobilize agreement while remaining too indeterminate to guide contested decisions. The same pattern recurs across the principle landscape. Fairness is a particularly revealing case. In moral and political philosophy, fairness is not a settled criterion but the beginning of a normative debate: it may refer to equal treatment, equal outcomes, proportionality, procedural impartiality, the correction of historical disadvantage, or the distribution of benefits and burdens under background conditions of inequality. AI ethics guidelines often invoke the term as if it were already action-guiding. Yet the academic literature itself shows how unstable this assumption is. Reviewing 47 AI ethics guidelines, Attard-Frost et al. [ 2 ] find that fairness and accountability are among the most frequently addressed principles, but that fairness in AI business practices is distributed across divergent themes such as open innovation, market fairness, and bias and diversity in professional practices. This is not merely terminological variation. It shows that the same principle can attach to different objects of evaluation and different normative concerns. In this respect, calls for “fair AI” can perform the kind of ethical assurance that critical scholars associate with “ethics washing”: They signal moral seriousness while leaving unresolved which conception of fairness, which site of application, and which criteria of failure are at stake. Metcalf et al. [ 21 ] make a complementary point at the organizational level. As ethics becomes institutionalized in technology companies as roles, procedures, and practices, there is a risk that open-ended questions of human value are prematurely foreclosed. Accountability is similarly affirmed without clarifying who should be accountable to whom, for which decisions, and under what sanctions. Trustworthiness is treated as a goal without distinguishing between warranted trust, misplaced trust, and strategic trust-building. In each case, the principle appears ethically attractive because it is insulated from concrete disagreement. The most influential global frameworks exemplify this dynamic. UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence articulates an ambitious set of values intended to be globally acceptable [ 36 ]. The OECD’s AI principles similarly emphasize human-centered values, transparency, and robustness, while explicitly seeking compatibility with innovation and economic growth [ 27 ]. The European Commission’s Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI frame ethical AI as lawful, ethical, and robust, supported by a set of key requirements [ 5 ]. These documents are normatively serious and politically significant. Yet their shared reliance on high-level principles also illustrates the central difficulty. They largely avoid explicit prioritization and rarely specify how conflicts between endorsed values are to be resolved. This avoidance is often strategic. Explicit prioritization risks alienating stakeholders who endorse the principle but disagree about its implications. By contrast, abstract formulations enable broad coalitions. But the ethical cost of this strategy is that principles remain compatible with mutually incompatible practices. A system can be defended as fair under one interpretation and criticized as unfair under another, without any shared criteria for adjudication. Ethical language thus becomes a medium of legitimation rather than a mechanism of constraint. Footnote 1 Examples of this dynamic are not hard to find. In the domain of generative AI, competing conceptions of fairness can justify mutually incompatible outputs: a model may be criticized as biased for reproducing occupational gender disparities, yet it may also be criticized as misleading or politically manipulative when it departs from perceived reality. Richter et al. [ 31 ] make this conflict vivid by showing that users often prefer generative AI to depict what they take to be real rather than what ethicists might regard as ideal, underscoring how the same “fairness” label can mask incompatible standards of evaluation. The point is not which standard is correct, but that the guideline label itself does not decide between them. Absent further specification, the same guideline can therefore be used to legitimate opposing outputs, with neither side forced to say what would count as failure. Trade-offs are not an unfortunate side effect of ethical reasoning in AI contexts; they are its defining test. Whenever multiple values are jointly endorsed, situations will arise in which they cannot all be fully realized at once. In such cases, ethical guidance must do more than restate commitments—it must indicate how to navigate conflict. The tendency of many guidelines to downplay trade-offs is therefore diagnostic. Documents often imply that fairness, transparency, privacy, safety, and innovation can be jointly maximized, or that conflicts can be resolved through better design alone. Yet extensive work on sociotechnical systems shows that abstractions introduced to satisfy one normative criterion can undermine others [ 32 ]. For example, formal fairness constraints may ignore relevant social context, while context-sensitive adjustments may violate formal notions of equality. These are not implementation glitches but structural tensions. A simple organizational analogy illustrates the point. Commitments to non-discrimination and to the active promotion of underrepresented groups are both widely endorsed in institutional ethics statements. Yet they can conflict in practice: policies designed to correct structural disadvantage may involve differential treatment that appears inconsistent with strict non-discrimination. Whether such policies are justified depends on how one weighs competing interpretations of fairness and equality. An ethics framework that treats both commitments as unproblematic goods, without articulating the conditions under which one may override the other, fails to guide judgment when it matters most. Recent empirical work reinforces this diagnosis from a different angle. Jauernig et al. [ 13 ] show that people often prefer decision-makers who retain moral discretion over systems that rigidly apply fairness rules, even when those rules are transparent and well-defined. Their experiments suggest that moral acceptability is closely tied to the perceived ability to respond to context and to override principles when warranted. This preference does not reflect a rejection of fairness as such, but a resistance to its unqualified, rule-bound application. The finding supports a broader point: ethical principles acquire meaning through their handling of exceptions and conflicts, not through their abstract formulation. The implication is not that ethics should abandon principles in favor of unstructured discretion. Rather, it is that principles must be embedded in a framework that makes their limits explicit. Trade-offs force ethical concepts to reveal their substantive commitments. When guidelines avoid trade-offs, they also avoid taking responsibility for the normative judgments implicit in design and governance choices. A concrete illustration of this problem, that is not necessarily limited to AI, can be found in public diversity commitments of large organizations. Mercedes-Benz, for instance, states that it seeks to attract and develop professionals “regardless of … gender and gender identity,” while adding, in the same statement, that it “actively promote[s] the advancement of women” [ 20 ]. Taken at face value, these commitments are normatively incompatible: the former excludes gender as a relevant decision criterion, while the latter explicitly reintroduces it. This is an even more basic failure than underdetermination in the management of trade-offs: it is a failure of minimal consistency. If the commitments are read in their natural sense, they cannot be jointly satisfied. This also helps explain why guideline-style normativity is often formulated at a high level of abstraction. Vagueness is strategically attractive because it allows institutions to signal multiple commitments while avoiding the point at which those commitments become mutually inconsistent and thus plainly criticizable. Their apparent compatibility depends on additional unstated assumptions about temporal sequencing, decision levels, or justificatory aims. Without explicit criteria for prioritization and application, such normative statements cannot guide judgment in cases of conflict. They merely affirm values while leaving their practical implications indeterminate. Anthropic’s constitutional framework for Claude provides a more recent and technologically specific illustration of a related problem. Unlike many high-level AI ethics statements, the constitution attempts to operationalize ethical commitments through behavioral guidance, refusal policies, and explicit priority relations between values such as helpfulness, harmlessness, honesty, and broader safety considerations. Yet the example is instructive precisely because greater specification does not eliminate underdetermination. The document repeatedly relies on contextual interpretation and holistic judgment where concrete conflicts arise, for instance when helpfulness risks becoming dangerous assistance, when honesty conflicts with harm prevention, or when safety considerations justify paternalistic refusal. Anthropic explicitly acknowledges that the constitution may in some cases remain “unclear, underspecified, or even contradictory”, requiring interpretation of the “spirit of the document” rather than mechanical rule application [ 1 ], p. 9). The significance of the example therefore lies not in a failure of implementation, but in the fact that even comparatively elaborate attempts to translate ethical principles into operational governance frameworks continue to depend on discretionary judgments concerning acceptable risk, behavioral thresholds, and the relative priority of competing normative commitments. What appears as increased ethical determinacy thus often turns out to be a redistribution of normative discretion into the interpretation and administration of behavioral constraints, risk thresholds, and acceptable forms of model conduct. One might object that underdetermined ethical concepts are harmless precisely because they lack binding force. If principles are vague, perhaps they simply leave room for local interpretation and democratic deliberation. This objection underestimates the social power of ethical language. Normative concepts can exert influence even when they are conceptually unstable. Empirical evidence illustrates this risk. Kruegel et al. [ 18 ] show that people are influenced by moral advice generated by large language models, even when that advice is inconsistent and even when users are explicitly informed about the artificial nature of the source. The system’s moral outputs lack a coherent underlying normative theory, yet they nonetheless shape judgments and decisions. The problem here is not that the advice is “wrong” in any straightforward sense, but that it acquires de facto moral authority without being anchored in determinate commitments that could be scrutinized or contested. This finding underscores a general concern: normative indeterminacy does not neutralize normative power. On the contrary, it can make ethical claims more difficult to challenge, precisely because there is no clear target for criticism. When ethical language functions as an influence mechanism rather than as a justificatory framework, accountability is weakened. Who is responsible for a recommendation that can always be reinterpreted as compatible with the underlying values? The relevance of this point extends beyond AI systems that generate moral advice. Guidelines, corporate principles, and institutional ethics statements can shape expectations, allocate blame, and legitimate decisions without specifying the standards by which those decisions should be assessed. In such cases, ethical discourse risks becoming a source of normative noise: influential enough to matter, but too indeterminate to discipline action. The analysis in this section supports a restrained but demanding conclusion. Ethical concepts seem to be indispensable to AI governance, but only under conditions that make them criticizable. High-level principles that avoid specification, prioritization, and conflict may facilitate consensus, but they do so at the cost of normative substance. Avoiding conflicts is therefore not a way of doing ethics more carefully, but a way of insulating ethical claims from criticism by leaving no determinate standard that could be contested or shown to have failed. Trade-offs are not a failure of ethics. They are the point at which ethics becomes informative. When guidelines obscure or suppress these tensions, they undermine their own claim to guide responsible practice. This diagnosis does not imply that ethical disagreement can be eliminated, nor that principles can be mechanically applied. It implies instead that ethical discourse must expose, rather than conceal, the judgments it presupposes. Without such exposure, ethics risks becoming a vocabulary of approval detached from the realities it purports to govern. The difficulty, however, is not exhausted by the fact that high-level principles often fail to determine action on their own. Even where ethical commitments are further specified through governance frameworks, technical standards, institutional procedures, or behavioral constraints, additional forms of underdetermination emerge at the level of implementation itself. Questions concerning acceptable risk, appropriate metrics, meaningful oversight, system boundaries, downstream effects, and contexts of deployment cannot be resolved through abstract principles alone. They depend on empirical assumptions about how sociotechnical systems function in practice, how harms materialize, which trade-offs are institutionally tolerated, and which forms of evidence are treated as sufficient. The problem is therefore not simply that ethical principles remain vague, but that their practical realization depends on further judgments that are simultaneously normative, technical, organizational, and empirical. 3 Normativity and the problem of implementation The preceding section argued that ethical concepts in AI governance must be sufficiently determinate to be criticizable and action-guiding. The remainder of the argument concerns a second condition: Ethical guidance must also remain connected to the institutional, technical, and empirical conditions under which it is enacted. Even normatively well-specified ethical commitments can be insufficient if they abstract from the conditions under which they are meant to guide action. Ethical guidance for AI systems is not addressed to an idealized world of frictionless choice, but to concrete institutional, technical, and social contexts. Ignoring these contexts does not preserve the purity of normativity; it undermines its plausibility. This section develops a second, complementary claim: normative AI ethics must take implementation seriously, and it can do so only by engaging with empirical insights about how ethical principles are instantiated, mediated, and transformed in practice. This is not an argument for replacing normative reasoning with empirical analysis, nor for deriving values from facts. Rather, it is an argument that normative claims about what ought to be done in AI contexts implicitly presuppose assumptions about feasibility, behavior, and institutional response. When those assumptions are left unexamined, ethical guidance risks becoming normatively irresponsible. This second form of underdetermination should not be confused with the ordinary fact that principles require local interpretation. The issue is that normative claims about AI systems often contain implicit empirical premises about feasibility, institutional behavior, measurement, user response, and downstream effects. If these premises are false or idealized, the ethical recommendation can fail even where the underlying principle is conceptually clear. Normative claims that purport to guide the design or deployment of AI systems are not merely evaluative, they are implicitly practical. To say that an AI system ought to be fair, transparent, or accountable is to imply that there exists some feasible course of action through which these properties can be realized. This intuition is familiar from broader debates in moral philosophy, often captured by the idea that “ought” implies “can” [ 34 , 35 ]. While the scope and interpretation of this principle remain contested, its relevance here is limited but clear: ethical guidance that systematically ignores constraints of implementation cannot plausibly claim authority over practice. In AI ethics, this point is frequently acknowledged but rarely pursued. Guidelines often gesture toward implementation by calling for “appropriate measures,” “adequate safeguards,” or “effective oversight,” without specifying what these terms require in concrete organizational or technical settings [ 4 ]. The result is a gap between normative aspiration and operational reality. Ethical principles are articulated at a level that allows them to be endorsed independently of any clear account of how they would constrain actual decisions. This gap is not accidental. High-level formulations are often chosen precisely because they travel well across contexts. Yet the cost of such portability is that responsibility for interpretation and enactment is deferred downstream—to developers, organizations, or end users—without corresponding guidance on how normative conflicts should be managed. Ethics, in this form, risks becoming an upstream declaration paired with downstream discretion, rather than a framework that meaningfully structures decision-making. Treating implementation as normatively irrelevant is not a neutral choice. How ethical principles are translated into technical and organizational practices shapes their meaning and effects. Implementation determines which values are prioritized, which risks are tolerated, and which groups bear the costs of error. For this reason, implementation cannot be treated as a purely technical afterthought to normative analysis. Recent work has begun to make this point explicit. Morley et al. [ 26 ] survey a growing ecosystem of tools and methods—such as impact assessments, documentation practices, and auditing frameworks—designed to translate ethical principles into operational processes. Their review shows both the promise and the limits of such tools. On the one hand, they make ethical commitments more concrete by embedding them in workflows, artifacts, and decision points. On the other hand, they reveal how quickly ethical aspirations are reshaped by organizational incentives, resource constraints, and measurement practices. Contemporary responsible-AI governance frameworks make this dynamic particularly visible. Microsoft’s responsible-AI materials provide a useful example. The 2022 Responsible AI Standard translates abstract commitments into operational processes through impact assessments, documentation requirements, responsible release criteria, evaluation plans, and monitoring obligations (Microsoft [ 22 ]). The 2025 Responsible AI Transparency Report extends this procedural architecture through red-teaming practices, deployment safety reviews, incident-response mechanisms, and post-deployment monitoring (Microsoft [ 23 ]). Yet the introduction of such mechanisms does not eliminate the underlying normative problem. Rather, it shifts ethical judgment into questions concerning measurement, thresholds, and institutional interpretation: which harms count as sufficiently serious, which populations are represented during testing, what level of residual risk is acceptable, or when deployment should be delayed, modified, or withdrawn. The resulting governance structures may render ethical commitments more administratively actionable, but they do not remove the dependence of those commitments on discretionary judgments about evidence, trade-offs, and acceptable uncertainty. In this sense, implementation frameworks risk reproducing a central problem of guideline-style ethics: they create increasingly elaborate procedures for managing ethical commitments without fully resolving the normative indeterminacy on which those procedures continue to depend. Empirical evidence further complicates the picture. Kruegel et al. [ 17 ] demonstrate that human oversight and transparency do not reliably mitigate undue trust in AI advice. In experiments, participants continued to rely on AI recommendations even when informed that the systems were trained on normatively problematic data or produced unreliable outputs. The presence of a “human in the loop” did not function as the safeguard often imagined in ethical and policy discourse. These findings challenge a common assumption in AI ethics: that transparency and oversight, once provided, will be taken up in normatively appropriate ways. The lesson is not that transparency or oversight are misguided, but that normative functions depend on how they interact with human behavior and institutional context. Ethical principles that assume idealized forms of user engagement or organizational response risk mischaracterizing the effects of their own implementation. Normative guidance that abstracts from these dynamics is incomplete in a way that matters ethically. Recognizing the normative relevance of implementation does not require abandoning the distinction between facts and values. Instead, it points toward a more integrated relationship between normative ethics and empirical inquiry. A useful analogue can be found in debates about moral intuitions. Singer [ 33 ], drawing on work associated with Haidt[ 10 ] and Greene[ 8 ], argues that empirical research in moral psychology and neuroscience does not directly settle normative questions, but it can undermine excessive confidence in certain forms of moral reasoning by revealing the contingent sources of the intuitions on which they rely. The relevance of empirical inquiry to AI ethics is similar. It does not derive values from facts; rather, it tests whether the background assumptions that give normative claims practical force are plausible under actual sociotechnical conditions. Behavioral ethics reinforces this point by showing that moral conduct is shaped by costs, incentives, group norms, institutional environments, and culturally variable expectations, rather than by abstract endorsement alone [ 7 ]. In bioethics, this relationship has been explored under the heading of “empirical ethics,” which investigates how empirical findings can inform, challenge, and refine normative analysis without reducing to descriptivism (cf. [ 3 , 11 , 25 ]). The relevance of this approach to AI ethics lies in its feedback function. Empirical studies can reveal how ethical concepts are interpreted, enacted, and experienced in practice. They can identify unintended consequences, distributional effects, and behavioral responses that were not anticipated at the level of principle. Such findings do not settle normative questions, but they can expose tensions between ethical aspirations and their real-world instantiations. A useful way to frame this relationship is through the structure of practical reasoning. This point can be expressed in the familiar form of a practical syllogism: a normative premise specifies an end or constraint, while an empirical premise specifies the means-end relation under the relevant institutional and technical conditions. On this view, the practical force of “ethical AI” claims depends on whether their implicit empirical premises are true—and revisable when they are not. Scholars in business ethics have used practical syllogisms precisely to diagnose how “folk-ethical” claims can retain rhetorical force while remaining resistant to correction [ 29 ]. Normative premises specify goals or constraints,empirical premises describe the conditions under which actions produce effects. Together, they inform judgments about what ought to be done in a given context. If empirical premises are systematically ignored or idealized, normative guidelines lose their guidance on practice. Ethical guidance then risks recommending courses of action that are infeasible, counterproductive, or normatively perverse once implemented. This perspective helps clarify the role of empirical work in AI ethics. Empirical studies are not merely illustrative add-ons to normative arguments. They test the background assumptions that normative claims rely on. When those assumptions prove false—as in cases of persistent overtrust or misaligned incentives—normative guidance must be revised or qualified accordingly. To refuse such revision in the name of preserving normative purity is to misunderstand the source of normativity’s authority in applied contexts. These considerations cast a different light on the role of ethics guidelines. Documents such as those issued by UNESCO, the OECD, and the European Commission have significant agenda-setting power. They articulate shared values and signal political commitment. Yet their normative force depends on how their principles are operationalized and contested in practice. If guidelines remain at the level of abstract commitment, they risk functioning as symbolic assurances rather than as constraints on action. If, by contrast, they are treated as inputs into ongoing processes of specification, testing, and revision—processes informed by empirical evidence about their effects—they can play a more constructive role. This does not require transforming ethics into regulation or reducing normative judgment to metrics. It requires acknowledging that ethical guidance aimed at sociotechnical systems must be responsive to how those systems actually operate. Such responsiveness has at least three functions. It tests assumptions about feasibility and effects. It identifies unintended consequences and distributional burdens. And it supplies feedback for revising the thresholds, metrics, and institutional procedures through which principles are applied. Work on AI and behavioral ethics provides a concrete example of what such responsiveness can look like. Koebis et al. [ 15 ] do not simply call for more “responsible AI” in general terms. They distinguish the roles through which AI agents can influence human ethical behavior, as role models, advisors, partners, and delegates, and derive from this distinction a research agenda for evidence-based oversight. Their analysis shows why apparently plausible safeguards, such as transparency about algorithmic presence, require empirical testing rather than intuitive endorsement, since their effects may vary depending on the social role the AI system occupies. Kruegel et al. [ 19 ] make the point still more concrete. Their experiments suggest that human-in-the-loop arrangements cannot be treated as ethically adequate merely because a human is formally present. When the algorithm decides and the human is expected to correct it, participants often take advantage of the algorithm’s bad decision rather than correct it; by contrast, systems in which the human decides and the algorithm serves as corrective appear more promising. This is precisely the kind of empirical feedback that implementation-sensitive ethics requires. It does not settle the value of fairness, responsibility, or oversight, but it can rule out naïve institutional assumptions about how such values will be realized in practice. Normative AI ethics cannot discharge its responsibilities by articulating values and buzzwords alone. It must also attend to the pathways through which those values are realized, distorted, or undermined in practice. Empirical, experimental, descriptive, and behavioral approaches therefore do not provide an external solution to normative disagreement. They discipline applied normative claims by making their background assumptions visible, revisable, and contestable. 4 Conclusion This paper examined a tension at the center of contemporary AI ethics. Ethical principles and guidelines have become widely endorsed and institutionally influential, yet their capacity to guide judgment and ground responsibility remains limited. Rather than attributing this limitation to the choice of particular values, the analysis focused on more basic conditions under which normative ethical concepts can meaningfully operate in contexts shaped by sociotechnical complexity. Two claims structured the argument. The first concerned the conditions of normative critique. Ethical concepts do not acquire normative force merely by being morally appealing or broadly accepted. They become action-guiding only when they are sufficiently determinate to support criticism—when it is possible to say not only what counts as compliance, but also what would count as failure. Concepts that remain elastic or tautological may facilitate agreement, but they do so by suspending precisely the kinds of judgments that ethics is meant to make. Trade-offs play a diagnostic role here. Where values conflict, ethical concepts are forced to reveal their substantive commitments. When ethical discourse avoids such conflicts, it risks functioning as a language of affirmation rather than as a framework for judgment. The second claim concerned the relation between normativity and implementation. Even conceptually well-specified ethical commitments cannot plausibly govern AI systems if they abstract from the conditions under which they are enacted. Normative claims about what ought to be done implicitly rely on assumptions about feasibility, institutional behavior, and human response. When these assumptions remain implicit or idealized, ethical guidance risks becoming normatively irresponsible: it may recommend actions that fail to constrain practice, or that produce effects at odds with the values they are meant to realize. Taken together, these claims support a restrained conclusion. Much of what currently operates under the label of “AI ethics” is normatively thinner than it appears—not because the values invoked are misguided, but because they are insufficiently specified and insufficiently connected to the realities of implementation. This diagnosis does not imply that ethical disagreement can be eliminated, nor that normative judgment can be replaced by technical procedures. It does, however, suggest that ethical guidance which avoids prioritization, conflict, and responsibility for enactment cannot plausibly claim authority over sociotechnical systems that are themselves shaped by constraint, trade-off, and institutional mediation. It is equally important to note what does not follow from this analysis. The paper does not offer a decision procedure for resolving ethical conflicts, nor does it defend a particular substantive theory of justice or fairness. Disagreement at that level is unavoidable and, in many cases, appropriate. Nor does the argument suggest that ethical guidance can be reduced to metrics, audits, or compliance tools. Such instruments may make normative commitments more visible and contestable, but they do not substitute for ethical judgment. The contribution of the paper lies instead in articulating minimal conditions for responsible normative discourse in AI contexts. These conditions should not be understood as a demand that ethical principles become mechanically decisive. Rather, they require that the judgments principles leave open about priority, evidence, feasibility, measurement, and institutional responsibility, be made sufficiently explicit to be criticized. Ethical concepts must be capable of being contested; they must expose, rather than conceal, the judgments they presuppose; and they must acknowledge their dependence on the empirical conditions of their own realization. Where these conditions are met, ethics can fulfill its critical role—structuring disagreement, clarifying responsibility, and constraining justification. Where they are not, ethics risks becoming symbolically powerful but normatively inert. If AI ethics is to move beyond buzzwords without abandoning its normative ambition, it must accept a degree of discomfort. Conceptual clarity brings conflict into view; attention to implementation exposes limits and failures. Neither guarantees moral agreement, but both are prerequisites for ethical seriousness. The alternative—normativity without criteria and without feedback—may be rhetorically attractive, but it cannot bear the normative weight increasingly placed upon it. He argues that contemporary AI ethics is shaped by institutional incentive structures that reward problem-highlighting and high-level normative critique while disincentivizing the specification of criteria, priorities, or mechanisms of resolution. As a result, ethical discourse can become systematically detached from action-guiding constraints without relying on bad faith or conceptual confusion. While Koenigs’ analysis targets the dynamics of the field itself, it supports the present claim that ethical language may retain legitimating force even when it lacks the structure required to guide judgment.
