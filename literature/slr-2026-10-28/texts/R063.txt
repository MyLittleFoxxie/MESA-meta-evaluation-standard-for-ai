Abstract 
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly evaluated across ophthalmic tasks, yet the 
methodological quality of these evaluations has received little attention. Most studies report 
accuracy while overlooking design features required for valid and clinically meaningful inference. 
We critically reviewed published ophthalmic LLM evaluation studies covering knowledge 
assessment, patient education, diagnostic reasoning, triage, clinical documentation, multimodal 
image interpretation, and uncertainty estimatio n. We used the methodological weaknesses that 
recurred across study designs to develop the SIGHT framework, which has five domains: Sample 
and item provenance, Independence and blinding, Ground truth and comparator fairness, Handling 
of paired and repeated-measures data, and Transparency and trustworthiness. Current evaluations 
rely heavily on benchmark accuracy, synthetic clinical vignettes, and curated datasets, and real -
world clinical validation remains rare. Common limitations include potential training -data 
contamination, inadequate handling of paired data, limited assessment of inter -rater reliability, 
inconsistent reference standards, insufficient evaluation of calibration and uncertainty, and 
frequent overinterpretation of benchmark performance as ev idence of clinical readiness. SIGHT 
provides twenty structured appraisal items to evaluate internal validity, interpretability, and 
applicability across diverse ophthalmic LLM study designs. It offers a practical way to appraise 
ophthalmic LLM studies and may improve the methodological quality, interpretability, and clinical 
relevance of future evaluations. 
Keywords: Large language models; Ophthalmology; Critical appraisal; Risk of bias; Evaluation 
methodology; Uncertainty quantification.

1. Introduction 
Ophthalmology adopted artificial intelligence (AI) early and with unusual confidence. 
Convolutional neural networks for diabetic retinopathy screening moved from proof-of-concept to 
regulatory clearance within a few years  (Abràmoff et al., 2018; Gulshan et al., 2016; Ipp et al., 
2021), and the specialty developed, comparatively quickly, an evidentiary culture around image -
based AI: labelled datasets (De Fauw et al., 2018) , external validation cohorts  (Lee et al., 2021; 
Ting et al., 2017) , prospective screening trials  (Ruamviboonsuk et al., 2022; Wolf et al., 2024) , 
and eventually regulatory pathways. That culture has not transferred to large language models 
(LLMs). 
Since late 2022, general-purpose LLMs have been evaluated against board examinations, patient 
questions, clinical vignettes, referral letters, operative notes, imaging cases and educational 
materials(Kung et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023; Singhal et al., 2023; Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023) . 
The volume is remarkable. One systematic review identified 187 quantitative ophthalmic LLM 
evaluations published up to November 2024 (Zhang et al., 2025) ; a broader medical review 
counted 519 LLM evaluations across all of medicine to February 2024, of which only 5% used 
real patient care data (Bedi et al., 2025) . Bibliometric analysis of first -quartile ophthalmology 
journals alone identified 101 eligible articles by mid -2024, of which roughly one third were 
original investigations (Agnihotri et al., 2025). 
This literature has been repeatedly mapped. Zhang et al. classified ophthalmic LLM evaluations 
into six domains, medical query answering, standardized examinations, diagnostic decision -
making, research assistance, patient triaging, and disease prediction (Zhang et al., 2025). Scoping 
and systematic reviews now exist for glaucoma, for primary -care ophthalmic education, and for 

=== PDF PAGE 5 ===
vision and vision -language foundation models (Jin et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Rubegni et al., 
2026). A reader arriving at this field today is not short of maps(Artiaga et al., 2025). 
What the field lacks is a critical reading. The existing syntheses answer the question what has been 
studied? with considerable rigor. They do not answer the question that matters for clinical 
adoption: given how these studies were designed, what are we actually entitled to believe(Agrawal 
et al., 2025)? 
An exploratory meta -analysis illustrates the problem. Despite reporting a pooled diagnostic 
accuracy of 59.4%, heterogeneity was extremely high (I² = 94.5%), and subgroup analysis 
unexpectedly suggested that GPT -3.5 outperformed GPT -4 (Zhang et al., 2025) . This unlikely 
finding is best interpreted as evidence of methodological inconsistency rather than a true difference 
in model performance. In other words, variation in how studies are conducted currently exceeds 
the signal we are trying to measure, highlighting the need for better study design and standardized 
evaluation rather than simply more studies. 
This review therefore takes a different organizing principle from its predecessors. We survey 
performance evidence domain by domain, as others have. But at each domain we ask what design 
features would be required to support the claims being made, and whether they are present. We 
then consolidate these ob servations into a five -domain appraisal instrument (the SIGHT 
framework) intended for use by investigators designing studies, by peer reviewers assessing them, 
and by future systematic reviewers weighting them. 
Our central argument is that evaluation design may be an increasingly important bottleneck in 
translating LLMs into ophthalmic care, as model capabilities continue to evolve rapidly, while 
evaluation methodologies may not have kept pace with these advances . The methodological

2. Methods 
2.1 Review design 
This is a structured narrative review, assessed using with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative 
Review Articles (SANRA) quality criteria  (Baethge et al., 2019) . A narrative rather than 
systematic format was chosen deliberately: three systematic reviews and one meta-analysis of this 
literature already exist, and the marginal value of a fourth enumeration is low  (Li et al., 2026; 
Rubegni et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025) . The contribution intended here is 
interpretive and methodological, which a narrative structure accommodates and a structure 
constrained by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta -Analyses 
(PRISMA) statement does not (Greenhalgh et al., 2018). 
2.2 Search strategy 
We searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar for English-language literature 
published between January 2022 and  July 2026 , combining terms for large language models 
("large language model", "LLM", "ChatGPT", "GPT-4", "Gemini", "Claude", "generative artificial 
intelligence", "chatbot", "foundation model") with terms for ophthalmology and its subspecialties. 
Reference lists of  identified reviews were hand -searched, and forward citation chaining was 
performed on the four principal syntheses  (Li et al., 2026; Rubegni et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2024; 
Zhang et al., 2025). 

=== PDF PAGE 8 ===
We supplemented this with targeted searches of the methodological literature on LLM evaluation, 
including reporting guidelines registered with the Enhancing the QUAlity and Transparency Of 
health Research (EQUATOR) Network (Collaborative, 2025; Collins et al., 2024; Gallifant et al., 
2025; Liu et al., 2020; Vasey et al., 2022). 
2.3 Development of the SIGHT appraisal framework 
Until recently, no risk-of-bias instrument existed for medical LLM evaluation studies, an absence 
explicitly acknowledged by the largest systematic review in this space, which was obliged to 
weight all included studies equally irrespective of methodological quality (Zhang et al., 2025) . 
That gap has now been partly filled. The Alberta Quality Assessment Tool: Risk of Bias 
(AQAT:RoB), developed through modified Delphi with 19 panellists and pilot -validated on 16 
studies (86.1% agreement, Cohen κ 0.70), assesses risk of bias in medical LL M question-answer 
studies across five domains: Questions, Reference Answers, LLM Answers, Evaluators, and 
Outcomes (Ye et al., 2026). 
AQAT:RoB is the appropriate foundation, and SIGHT is constructed to complement rather than 
duplicate it. Four gaps define the space SIGHT occupies, none of them specialty-specific. 
Eligible study designs.  AQAT:RoB applies to question-answer studies "that involve human 
participants in assessing the outputs of AI models." This excludes automated -scoring benchmark 
studies (the dominant design in standardized examination research) as well as multimodal image 
interpretation and documentation -generation tasks, none of which necessarily involve human 
rating of free-text output. As a result, at least 41 of the 187 studies (21.9%) identified in the largest 
systematic review of ophthalmic LLMs fall outside the types of studies AQAT:RoB is designed to 
evaluate (Zhang et al., 2025). 

=== PDF PAGE 9 ===
Statistical validity. AQAT:RoB contains no items addressing analysis. The paired and repeated -
measures structure that characterizes almost every multi -model comparison is therefore 
unassessed, as are multiplicity and effect -size reporting. A study can be judged low risk of bias 
throughout while having analyzed paired data with an independent-samples test. 
Reliability signals. Neither calibration (whether a model's confidence tracks its correctness) nor 
the faithfulness of model-stated reasoning is covered, and model version is not requested despite 
undisclosed snapshot revisions materially affecting reproducibility. 
Concordance between evidence and claims.  Risk-of-bias instruments assess whether a study's 
methods could have produced a biased result. They do not assess whether the conclusions drawn 
exceed what the methods can support , and in this literature that is where much of the damage 
occurs. A methodologically clean benchmark study that reports accuracy and concludes the model 
is "comparable to ophthalmologists" or "ready for clinical use" has committed no bias in 
AQAT:RoB's sens e, yet its central claim is unsupported. Several SIGHT items are therefore 
conditional on claim scope rather than on design alone: a study confining its conclusions to what 
it measured is marked not applicable, while one making deployment, human -comparability, or 
interpretability claims is assessed against the evidence those claims require. We therefore chose to 
address this explicitly. In the current LLM literature, unsupported conclusions are often more 
consequential than methodological flaws themselves, because they directly influence perceptions 
of clinical readiness and safety (Dong et al., 2026) . A model evaluated only on benchmark tasks 
may nevertheless be presented as comparable to clinicians or suitable for deployment, encouraging 
inappropriate translation into practice. SIGHT therefore evaluates not only methodological 
validity, but also whether a study's conclusions are justified by its evidence.  

=== PDF PAGE 10 ===
SIGHT therefore extends appraisal of LLM evaluation studies to designs and dimensions that 
existing instruments do not reach. Its items are general; we develop and illustrate them in 
ophthalmology because that literature is unusually mature, has been syste matically catalogued, 
and spans the full range of task types from examination benchmarking to multimodal 
interpretation.  
The resulting framework comprises five domains and twenty items (Figure 2); each item is 
specified in full in Section 4.

3. The evidence, domain by domain 
The current ophthalmic LLM literature spans a wide range of clinical applications, from 
knowledge examinations and patient education to diagnosis, triage, documentation, and 
multimodal image interpretation. For each domain, we summarize the available evidence and then 
examine the methodological issues that determine how confidently those findings can be 
interpreted. The emphasis is therefore not only on what LLMs can do, but also on how reliably 
current studies demonstrate those capabilities. 
Figure 3 summarizes the distribution of reported outcomes and task domains across the 187-study 
corpus that this section examines.

(whether a model's confidence corresponds to its correctness) was assessed in 2.7%, and only 2.1% 
involved real-world clinical deployment. Data from Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2025). 
 
3.1 Standardized examinations and ophthalmic knowledge assessment 
This is the most mature and most saturated domain in the field, and the one where further primary 
studies now add least (Antaki et al., 2023; Haddad & Saade, 2024; Lin et al., 2023; Raimondi et 
al., 2023; Taloni et al., 2023; Thirunavukarasu et al., 2024). 
The trajectory is well documented. Early evaluations of GPT -3.5 against ophthalmology board 
practice banks were unimpressive; Mihalache and colleagues found the model answered fewer 
than half of items correctly on a widely used preparation resource, with a re-analysis one month 


=== PDF PAGE 13 ===
later showing improvement to 58% (Mihalache et al., 2023) . Cai et al. compared three models 
against 250 Basic Science and Clinical Science Self-Assessment Program items and against human 
respondents: humans averaged 72.2%, GPT -3.5 scored 58.8%, and GPT -4.0 (71.6%) and Bing 
Chat (71.2%) performed comparably to hu mans (Cai et al., 2023) . Antaki et al. reported GPT -4 
exceeding 70% on simulated board -style examinations , not significantly higher than humans  
(Antaki et al., 2024) , and later work documented incremental gains on Ophthalmic Knowledge 
Assessment Program (OKAP)-style question banks  (Teebagy et al., 2023). 
Wu et al. meta -analyzed fourteen such studies and reported pooled accuracy of 0.65 ( 95% 
confidence interval [CI] 0.61–0.69), with GPT -4 and Bing Chat the top performers (Wu et al., 
2024). Their conclusion that performance is "acceptable but not exceptional" has held up. 
Two findings from this domain deserve to be carried forward rather than re-established: 
Subspecialty variance is large and consistent in direction.  Bard answered 62.4% of 250 
BoardVitals items correctly overall, but 84% of oculoplastic items and only 24% of retina and 
vitreous items (Botross et al., 2024) . This pattern (strong on descriptive, anatomically anchored 
subspecialties, weak on retina) recurs across studies and models and is unlikely to be an artefact 
of any single question bank (Galindo et al., 2026). 
Multiple-choice performance overstates competence.  The most methodologically informative 
recent study in this domain assessed both formats. Against ophthalmology continuing medical 
education questions, GPT -4 achieved 82.5% (99/120) on multiple choice and Gemini Pro 1.5 
49.2% (59/120). When the same models produced free-text prose answers assessed by three board-
certified ophthalmologists, the picture changed sharply: raters identified incorrect reasoning in 

=== PDF PAGE 14 ===
42% and 58% of responses, inappropriate content in 29% and 36%, missing content in 42% and 
30%, and possibility of physical or emotional harm in 36% and 44% (Chen et al., 2026). 
This study highlights a key limitation of the examination literature: strong performance on 
multiple-choice questions does not necessarily translate into safe clinical reasoning. A model may 
choose the correct answer yet provide incomplete, incorrect, or p otentially harmful explanations 
when asked to justify its decision. Therefore, board-style examination scores should be viewed as 
an indirect measure of clinical competence rather than evidence of safe real -world performance. 
Figure 4 shows this divergence directly: the same models and the same questions, with only the 
response format changed. 
 
Fig. 4. Multiple-choice accuracy does not predict the safety of the same model's free-text answers. 
Two models answered the same set of ophthalmic continuing medical education questions in two 


=== PDF PAGE 15 ===
response formats. In the closed -ended format, requiring selection of a single best answer, GPT -4 
answered 82.5 percent correctly and Gemini Pro 1.5 answered 49.2 percent correctly. When the 
same models produced open-ended free-text answers to the same questions, three board-certified

and the possibility of physical or emotional harm in 36 percent and 44 percent. Failure categories

2026). 
 
Appraisal note. Benchmark contamination. Almost no study in this domain addresses whether 
the item bank used appeared in model training data. Many commercial and educational question 
banks have substantial overlap with content available on the open web; Basic and Clinical Science 
Course (BCSC)-derived, OKAP-derived and StatPearls-derived items are plausibly present in pre-
training corpora. Contamination would inflate accuracy without any corresponding gain in 
reasoning ability, and would do so differentially across model generations with different training 
cutoffs. Contamination-resistant designs exist (newly written items, held -out items, perturbation 
of surface form with preserved clinical content) and are essentially unused. We regard this as one 
of the most important unexamined threats to validity in the examination literature (C. Deng et al., 
2024; Golchin & Surdeanu, 2024; Sainz et al., 2023) . 
3.2 Patient-facing question answering and patient education 

=== PDF PAGE 16 ===
This is the largest task category in the field, accounting for roughly 46% of studies in the Zhang 
et al. (Zhang et al., 2025) , and the domain with the clearest signal of genuine clinical 
relevance(Ayers et al., 2023; Cappellani et al., 2024; Tailor et al., 2024; Taloni et al., 2025). 
The clinical argument for LLMs in this domain rests substantially on access: a global shortage of 
ophthalmologists, unevenly distributed, against rising demand from ageing populations(Burton et 
al., 2021; Resnikoff et al., 2012; Resnikoff et al., 2020; Steinmetz et al., 2021) . If that is the case 
for deployment, then performance in low -resource settings and non -dominant languages is not a 
secondary consideration, it is the primary outcome. 
Bernstein et al. found that chatbot responses to online patient eye care questions were broadly 
comparable in quality to those written by ophthalmologists (Bernstein et al., 2023) . Subsequent 
work has replicated this across subspecialties: myopia care, conjunctivitis, dry eye disease in 
Chinese-language patients, age -related macular degeneration, childhood glaucoma education, 
optic disc drusen, orbital and oculofacial disorders, an d lacrimal drainage disorders , and even 
ocular symptom queries  or surgical treatments for retinal disease  (Ali, 2023; Balas et al., 2024; 
Dihan et al., 2024; Ferro Desideri et al., 2023; Lim et al., 2023; Momenaei et al., 2023; Potapenko 
et al., 2023; Pushpanathan et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). 
Three robust findings emerge. 
Content quality is generally adequate; readability is generally not.  ChatGPT-generated 
responses to common ophthalmology questions cluster around a tenth -grade reading level, and 
ChatGPT-4o responses to real patient portal messages averaged a Flesch –Kincaid grade level of 
12.1 ± 2.1 (Hamzeh et al., 2026; Riazi Esfahani et al., 2025) . Both sit far above the sixth - to 
seventh-grade level recommended for patient education materials(Edmunds et al., 2013; Williams 

=== PDF PAGE 17 ===
et al., 2016). LLM outputs are consistently less readable than professionally produced pamphlets 
even where they are more comprehensive. 
Language disparity. Non-English evaluation appeared in 12.8% of ophthalmic LLM studies, and 
of those, Chinese, Spanish and Japanese accounted for the large majority; the remaining languages 
appeared once each(Zhang et al., 2025). Where language disparity has been examined directly, it 
is substantial: performance on retinal vascular disease classification differs materially between 
English and Chinese prompts, and multilingual ophthalmic question-answering shows systematic 
degradation outside high-resource languages (Jin et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; P. Qiu et al., 2024; 
Restrepo et al., 2024; Sakai et al., 2023). 
Prompt-based readability optimization appears feasible without substantially compromising 
content quality.  In a study, instructing ChatGPT -4o to respond at a sixth -grade reading level 
improved readability while increasing the proportion of responses judged accurate and complete 
(85% vs. 77%) and reducing unacceptable responses (2% vs. 6%) (Hamzeh et al., 2026). Similarly, 
ChatGPT successfully simplified peer -reviewed ophthalmology articles from approximately a 
15th- to a 7th -grade reading level, with the rewritten content considered appropriate by expert 
ophthalmologists (Kianian et al., 2024) . The clinically actionable message , that readability is a 
prompt-engineering variable, not a fixed model property, is well supported. Figure 5 summarizes 
the gap between unprompted reading level and the recommended band, and what happened to 
content quality when the reading level was brought down by prompt instruction alone. 

=== PDF PAGE 18 ===

A major limitation of the current literature is that nearly all studies evaluate the quality of the 
generated response rather than its effect on patients. Whether LLM -generated education actually 


=== PDF PAGE 19 ===
improves patient comprehension, reduces anxiety, increases treatment adherence, or influences 
health-seeking behavior remains largely unknown. As a result, current evidence demonstrates that 
LLMs can generate acceptable educational content, but provides little evidence that this translates 
into better patient outcomes(Bean et al., 2026). 
Appraisal note . The comparator problem . A recurring limitation in the evaluation of LLM -
generated responses is the use of reference materials that differ substantially from the responses 
being assessed, including institutional patient education leaflets, previously documented clinician 
responses, or answers originally developed for patients with different clinical histories and 
informational needs. Such comparisons may introduce systematic biases because the responses are 
produced under different conditions and serve distinct communicative purpos es. Clinicians 
typically formulate advice within a specific clinical encounter, informed by the patient's medical 
history, individual circumstances, and contextual cues, whereas LLMs often generate responses to 
isolated questions without access to equivale nt patient -specific information. Consequently, 
observed differences in response quality may partly reflect disparities in the information available, 
the intended audience, and the purpose of the communication, rather than differences in the 
underlying clinical capabilities of the two sources. 
A further concern relates to the effectiveness of blinding in comparative assessments. Differences 
in writing style, response length, organization, and linguistic patterns may allow evaluators to infer 
whether a response was generated by an LLM or written by a clinician, even when the source is 
not explicitly disclosed. Such cues may influence judgments independently of clinical accuracy, 
completeness, or relevance. Therefore, nominal blinding does not necessarily ensure that 
assessments are free from sourc e-related bias. When reference materials are not sufficiently 
comparable and evaluators can potentially identify the origin of responses, the validity of between-

=== PDF PAGE 20 ===
source comparisons may be compromised. These methodological considerations highlight the 
importance of contextually appropriate reference standards, comparable assessment conditions, 
and rigorous blinding procedures when evaluating the clinical utility of LLM-generated responses. 
 
3.3 Diagnosis from clinical vignettes and case reports 
Diagnostic evaluation accounts for roughly 15% of the ophthalmic corpus (Zhang et al., 2025) and 
has been performed across  different diseases such as  glaucoma, corneal disease, neuro -
ophthalmology, uveitis, and rare eye diseases (Delsoz et al., 2024; Delsoz et al., 2023; Hu et al., 
2023; Madadi et al., 2024; Rojas-Carabali et al., 2024). 
Reported accuracies vary enormously, and the pooled estimate of 0.594 with I² = 94.5% is best 
read as a statement that these studies are not measuring the same thing. Subgroup pooling by 
subspecialty ranged from 0.483 (retina and vitreous) to 0.731 (external disease and cornea), with 
confidence intervals wide enough to overlap almost entirely (Zhang et al., 2025). 
Appraisal note . Vignettes are not patients.  Published case reports, the dominant source of 
diagnostic vignettes, have been written retrospectively by an author who already knows the 
diagnosis. The narrative is therefore pre-filtered for diagnostic relevance: irrelevant findings have 
been dropped, relevant findings foregrounded, and the differential implicitly narrowed. A model 
achieving 70% on such vignettes should not be interpreted as demonstrating 70% diagnostic 
accuracy in undifferentiated clinical presentations . Additionally, published cases are 
disproportionately unusual  which cuts in the opposite direction. Collectively, these features 
introduce substantial spectrum bias, limiting the generalizability of reported diagnostic 
performance to routine clinical practice(Kanjee et al., 2023; Mulherin & Miller, 2002; Ransohoff 

=== PDF PAGE 21 ===
& Feinstein, 1978) . These concerns are amplified by the limited use of real -world clinical 
data(Hager et al., 2024). Only about one-quarter of ophthalmic LLM studies used real patient data, 
and only 4 of 187 studies (2.1%) evaluated models within routine clinical practice rather than 
simulated scenarios (Zhang et al., 2025). Figure 6 traces what is discarded and what is emphasized 
as a real encounter is converted first into a published case report and then into a prompt. 
 
Fig. 6. How a clinical encounter becomes a prompt. At each step information is removed and other 
information is brought forward, so that part of the diagnostic work is already complete before the 
model receives any text. The real encounter is undifferentiated, in complete and uncertain; the 
author writes retrospectively, already knowing the diagnosis; the published case report presents a 
coherent and teachable narrative; and the prompt preserves only that narrative. Performance on 
such items therefore measures reas oning over curated clinical narratives rather than diagnostic

performance in undifferentiated presentations. This is a form of spectrum bias, meaning that the 
mix of cases tested differs systematically from the mix encountered in the setting where the system 
would be used. Only 4 of 187 ophthalmic large language model evaluations (2.1 percent) assessed

(Zhang et al., 2025). 
 
3.4 Triage and urgency assessment 
Patient triaging accounted for 1.6% of studies in the Zhang corpus (Zhang et al., 2025) . This is 
where LLMs would deliver most value in resource -constrained systems, and where the evidence 
base is thinnest. 
Early work compared GPT -4 and Bard on common ophthalmic complaints and assessed chatbot 
triage of ophthalmic conditions against clinician judgement (Lyons et al., 2024; Zandi et al., 2024). 
More recent studies are considerably stronger. Cardoso -Teixeira et al. evaluated five model 
systems on 3,831 real anonymized Portuguese-language referral vignettes across three 
independent runs, reporting baseline classification accuracy of 68.7% rising to 73.4% after 
supervised in -context learning, with peak accuracy of 79.5% and , importantly, a large gain in 
consistency for one model (66.8% to 93.8%). Performance exceeded 90% for common referral 
categories and degraded on rarer ones (Cardoso-Teixeira et al., 2026). 
In another vignette-based comparative study, ChatGPT -5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude Opus 4.1 
demonstrated comparable performance across urgency triage,  and red flag recognition  of 40 
ophthalmic emergency scenarios, with no significant differences between models  (Mittal & 
Aggarwal, 2026). 

=== PDF PAGE 23 ===
Two design features of the Cardoso -Teixeira study deserve emulation and are otherwise almost 
unseen in this literature: real-world clinical text rather than synthetic vignettes, and repeated 
independent runs with consistency reported as a primary outcome.  Consistency is arguably more 
important than accuracy for triage deployment . A  model that is right 80% of the time but 
unpredictably so is more dangerous in a referral pathway than one that is right 75% of the time 
reproducibly, because the latter's failure modes can be characterized and guarded. 
Appraisal note. Most studies treat all triage errors as equally important, even though they 
are not. Missing a sight -threatening condition such as acute angle -closure glaucoma, giant cell 
arteritis, retinal detachment, or endophthalmitis can lead to permanent vision loss, whereas 
unnecessarily referring a patient with a benign condition such as a chalazio n mainly results in an 
extra clinic visit. Despite this, most studies report only overall accuracy, which gives the same 
weight to both types of errors. Future studies should report sensitivity for sight -threatening 
conditions separately and, where possibl e, use evaluation metrics that assign greater weight to 
clinically serious errors. Equally important, future studies should evaluate whether LLM-assisted

3.5 Documentation and research support 
Documentation and research support represent relatively understudied but practically important 
applications of LLMs in ophthalmology. Unlike diagnostic or triage tasks, these applications 


=== PDF PAGE 25 ===
typically involve clinician oversight, potentially reducing the risks associated with erroneous 
outputs (Hua et al., 2023; Patel & Lam, 2023; Singh et al., 2023; Van Veen et al., 2024; Waisberg 
et al., 2023). 
The documentation use case is plausibly the nearest-term clinical value, precisely because it is the 
lowest-risk: a clinician reviews and signs the output, the error -detection burden falls on someone 
qualified to bear it, although automation bias can weaken this safeguard, even among experienced 
readers(Dratsch et al., 2023; Goddard et al., 2012) , and the counterfactual is a rushed human -
written note. Despite its potential for near -term implementation, documentation support has 
received relatively little attention compared with diagnostic benchmarking. 
Unlike documentation support, the literature on research assistance consistently identifies citation 
and content reliability as the major limitation. Across ophthalmology-specific and general medical 
evaluations, fabricated references, inaccurate bibliographic details, and  hallucinations remain 
common even in current-generation models(Alkaissi & McFarlane, 2023; Gravel et al., 2023). Hua 
et al. evaluated ophthalmic scientific abstracts and references generated by AI chatbots and 
documented reference fabrication (Hua et al., 2023). This is consistent with the broader literature: 
analysis of 636 citations across 84 GPT -3.5 and GPT -4 generated documents found substantial 
rates of both outright fabrication and citation error in non-fabricated references (Walters & Wilder, 
2023). An influential study also catalogued analyses reporting fabrication rates as high as 47% in 
generated medical references (Bhattacharyya et al., 2023) . Comparative work on systematic 
review support found hallucination rates of 39.6%, 28.6% and 91.4% for GPT -3.5, GPT-4 and 
Bard respectively (Chelli et al., 2024).  
Accordingly, LLM-generated references require manual verification against the original source, 
ideally using DOI -level validation, and LLMs should not be relied upon as the sole method of

3.6 Multimodal image interpretation 
Ophthalmology is an imaging specialty, yet this area has received surprisingly little research 
attention: only 10.2% of ophthalmic LLM studies involved images at all (Zhang et al., 2025). 


=== PDF PAGE 27 ===
Where multimodal capability has been tested, results are sobering. Xu et al. benchmarked GPT -
4V on ophthalmic multimodal image analysis and titled the resulting paper around its clinical 
incapabilities (Xu et al., 2024). Mihalache et al. assessed chatbot diagnosis of clinical ophthalmic 
images with consistently modest results (Andrew Mihalache et al., 2024; A. Mihalache et al., 
2024). Pooled image-text diagnostic accuracy in the Zhang meta-analysis was 0.407 versus 0.619 
for text-only tasks(Zhang et al., 2025). 
Another quantitative evaluation of four LLMs for diabetic retinopathy detection and grading from 
ultra-widefield fundus images, against Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) 
ground truth established by three retina specialists, found performance insufficient for clinical use 
across four prompting strategies (Most et al., 2026).  
It is important to separate two distinct technical lineages here, which the literature frequently 
conflates: 
• General-purpose multimodal LLMs (GPT-4V/4o, Gemini, Claude) have been tested on 
ophthalmic images in a zero-shot setting, meaning they were used without ophthalmology-
specific training. Their performance has generally been modest, which is not surprising 
because ophthalmic imaging is a highly specialized field that relies on subtle image details 
and is likely underrepresented in the large image-text datasets used to train these models. 
• Domain-specific vision -language and foundation models  RETFound, EyeCLIP, 
generalist ophthalmic vision foundation models, FFA -GPT and ChatFFA for fluorescein 
angiography interpretation, OphGLM, EYE -Llama, and integrated image -plus-language 
pipelines for primary diabetes care (Chen, Xu, et al., 2024; Chen, Zhang, et al., 2024; Z. 
Deng et al., 2024; Haghighi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; J. Qiu et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2025; 

=== PDF PAGE 28 ===
Zhou et al., 2023). These perform far better and represent the more plausible translational 
path (Sevgi et al., 2025) . Unlike general -purpose multimodal LLMs, these models are 
pretrained or fine -tuned on large ophthalmic imaging datasets, allowing them to learn 
disease-specific visual representations. 
Reviews that report "LLMs perform poorly on ophthalmic images" without this distinction are 
conflating a question about general-purpose chatbots with a question about purpose-built systems. 
The honest summary is: general -purpose multimodal models are not c urrently adequate for 
ophthalmic image interpretation; domain -specific vision -language models show substantially 
more promise but have themselves been evaluated almost entirely retrospectively on curated 
datasets. 
 
Appraisal note . Limited Evidence for Open -Source LLM Deployment in Ophthalmology . 
Open-source models have been evaluated in only 13.4% of ophthalmic LLM studies overall, 
although they were used in 77.3% of studies that developed or fine-tuned a model rather than only 
evaluating existing systems (Zhang et al., 2025). This limited attention is important because open-
weight models may be the most practical option for many healthcare systems, particularly those 
with limited resources or strict data privacy requirements. These models can be deployed locally 
within hospitals, reducing concerns related to data sharing and external cloud services(Riedemann 
et al., 2024; Wiest et al., 2024) . However, evidence regarding their performance and clinical 
applicability in ophthalmology remains limited, representing a clear opportunity for future 
research.

3.7 Uncertainty in ophthalmic LLMs 
Why uncertainty matters. Every finding in Sections 3.1 through 3.6 describes what these models 
get right on average. Few studies, however, assess whether a model can reliably distinguish cases 
in which its predictions are likely to be correct from those in which its outputs should be interpreted 
with caution. This represents a distinct dimension of model performance that cannot be adequately 
captured by accuracy alone  (Omar et al., 2025; Van Calster et al., 2019) . A  system that fails 
silently, at the moment it is least confident, converts a knowledge gap into a missed diagnosis. 
Calibration (the correspondence between a model's stated or implicit confidence and its actual 
correctness) was assessed in 2.7% of the 187 studies in the Zhang corpus, against accuracy 
reporting in 94.1% (Zhang et al., 2025) . The field has, in effect, measured what these systems 
know and almost never measured what they know they do not know. 
What uncertainty-aware LLMs should actually do. An uncertainty-aware LLM should do two 
things that current LLMs usually do not. 
First, it should distinguish between two different types of uncertainty. Aleatoric uncertainty comes 
from the case itself. For example, an image may be blurry, a lesion may have an unclear border, 
or even experts may disagree on the diagnosis. In these situations, uncertainty is expected because 
the available information is in herently limited. In contrast, epistemic uncertainty reflects the 
model's own lack of knowledge. This usually occurs when the model encounters rare diseases, 
unusual clinical presentations, or data that differ from what it has seen during training. Unlike 
aleatoric uncertainty, epistemic uncertainty can often be reduced by improving the model or 
providing more representative training data(Hüllermeier & Waegeman, 2021; Leibig et al., 2017). 

=== PDF PAGE 30 ===
These two types of uncertainty have different meanings. A model that is uncertain about an 
ambiguous fundus image may be behaving appropriately. However, if it is equally uncertain about 
a classic case of proliferative diabetic retinopathy, this suggests a  limitation of the model rather 
than a difficult case. Most reported only overall accuracy, making it impossible to determine 
whether the observed errors were caused by genuinely problematic, incomplete input or by gaps 
in the model's knowledge. 
Second, an uncertainty -aware LLM should know when not to answer confidently.  When 
confidence is low, the safest response is not to guess but to acknowledge the uncertainty and 
recommend human review or additional clinical evaluation(Dvijotham et al., 2023; Kompa et al., 
2021). For screening and triage applications, this should be considered a core safety feature rather 
than a limitation(Griot et al., 2025).  
 
Why current confidence measures fail . The confidence signals used in almost all ophthalmic 
LLM studies are the ones that the broader uncertainty -quantification literature considers least 
reliable(Gao et al., 2025; Savage et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2024). One common approach is to use 
the probability assigned to the generated words (tokens) as a measure of confidence. For example, 
repeated responses alternating between proliferative diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, 
and ocular ischemic syndrome indicate genuine diagnostic uncertainty because the model cannot 
consistently settle on the same diagnosis. Token -level confidence does not measure this. Instead, 
it measures the probability of individual generated words. Consequently, two responses expressing 
exactly the same diagnosis (for example, "proliferative diabetic retinopathy" and "the findings are 
most consistent with proliferative diabetic retinopathy" ) may receive different confidence 
estimates simply because they use different wording(Kuhn et al., 2023). 

=== PDF PAGE 31 ===
This helps explain a phenomenon already encountered throughout this review under different 
names. What the uncertainty -quantification literature refers to as medical hallucination (fluent, 
confident, but fabricated or unsupported output) is not a separate failure mode from the incorrect 
reasoning and fabricated references discussed earlier. Rather, these problems  share the same 
underlying cause: LLMs are trained to generate the most plausible continuation of text, not to 
determine whether the information is true (Kalai et al., 2025; Kuhn et al., 2023) . Consequently, a 
fabricated citation may look just as convincing as a genuine one, and an incorrect clinical 
explanation may be presented with the same confident language as a correct one. Recent work has 
shown that meaning-based uncertainty measures, such as semantic entropy, are substantially better 
at detecting these hallucinations than conventional token -level confidence because they measure 
uncertainty in the underlying meaning rather than in the exact wording of the response  (Farquhar 
et al., 2024). 
The same problem applies to the model's stated reasoning. Asking an LLM to explain its diagnosis 
produces fluent justification, but there is little basis for treating that justification as a faithful 
account of how the answer was actually produced (Turpin et al., 2023) . The study discussed in 
Section 3.1 is the clearest available evidence: GPT -4 selected the correct multiple-choice answer 
in 82.5% of items, yet expert raters identified incorrect reasoning in 42% of the free -text 
justifications the same model produced (Chen et al., 2026) . This matters because a confident, 
fluent, but unfaithful explanation is more dangerous than no explanation at all: an opaque model 
invites appropriate caution, whereas a plausible -looking rationale invites trust it has not 
earned(Ghassemi et al., 2021). 
 

=== PDF PAGE 32 ===
Possible solutions. Semantic entropy directly addresses the paraphrasing problem above and has 
not, to our knowledge, been applied in any ophthalmic evaluation. 
Conformal prediction may offer a practical and promising approach to addressing this limitation, 
although its application in the ophthalmic LLM literature appears to remain unexplored or 
insufficiently documented (Mohri & Hashimoto, 2024; Quach et al., 2024). Instead of one answer 
with an unreliable confidence score, it returns a prediction set with a formal statistical coverage 
guarantee: for a diagnostic task , a ranked shortlist of differentials designed to contain the correct 
diagnosis with a predefined statistical coverage (e.g. 95%) under appropriate assumptions.  This 
maps with unusual precision onto the diagnostic-vignette and triage tasks of Sections 3.3 and 3.4, 
where the clinically useful output may not be the model's single best guess but the set of 
possibilities that cannot yet be safely excluded.  
Proposed response framework for future ophthalmic LLMs. Rather than returning a diagnosis 
alone, future ophthalmic LLMs should provide outputs that explicitly communicate uncertainty 
and reliability.

4. Methodological appraisal: the SIGHT framework 
We constructed SIGHT inductively; from failure modes observed across the corpus reviewed in 
Section 3. It comprises five domains and twenty items: Sample and item provenance, 
Independence and blinding, Ground truth and comparator fairness, Handling of paired and 
repeated-measures data, and Transparency and trustworthiness. 
Scoring. Each item is scored Yes = 2, Partial = 1, No or Not reported (NR) = 0. Non-reporting is 
scored identically to non-compliance: a design feature that cannot be verified from the manuscript 
cannot be credited. Items deemed not applicable (NA) to a given study design are excluded from 
both the numerator and denominator. For each domain, the percentage score is calculated as the 
points earned divided by twice the number of applicable items, multiplied by 100. The overall 
SIGHT score is calculated as the unweighted mean of the domain percentage scores. The number 
of applicable items should be reported for each domain.  The maximum applicable score is 40, 
comprising S = 10, I = 6, G = 6, H = 8, T = 10. 
Interpreting and applying the profile. Eleven of the twenty items are NA-eligible, so applicable 
denominators vary by design. Two consequences follow, and both have practical remedies. 
First, because domains contain unequal numbers of items, raw summation implicitly weights the 
larger domains. Scores should therefore be domain -normalized: each domain expressed as a 
percentage of its own applicable maximum, and the overall score computed as the mean of domains 
with at least one applicable item.  
Second, domains are not exchangeable in what their failure implies. We designate H1 (analysis 
matched to data structure) and T1 (model version identified) as critical items: a score of 0 on 

=== PDF PAGE 35 ===
either classifies a study as high concern irrespective of its total, since the first invalidates the study's 
inferential claim and the second precludes replication. Overall judgment is therefore categorical ; 
low concern (≥70% domain-normalized, no critical item at 0, no domain below 40%), moderate 
concern (40–70%, or any domain below 40%), high concern (<40%, or any critical item at 0). 
 
4.1 S: Sample and item provenance 
S1. Are test items drawn from real clinical encounters, or synthesized? 
Synthetic and published -case items are not inherently inferior, but they measure a different 
construct: performance on curated clinical narratives in which information has already been 
selected, organized, and edited by someone who knows the diagnosis. Rea l encounters contain 
incomplete histories, irrelevant findings, and evolving presentations. 
• Yes (2): items derive from real clinical encounters. 
• Partial (1): items are synthetic, published -case-derived, or investigator -written, and the 
study explicitly limits its conclusions to curated -case performance rather than clinical 
performance. 
• No / NR (0) : synthetic or published -case items with conclusions generalized to clinical 
practice without qualification; or the source is described too vaguely to determine. 
 
S2. Has training-data contamination been handled? 

=== PDF PAGE 36 ===
Any study using a commercial question bank, published case series, or public patient -education 
website must address whether those items sit in the model's pre -training corpus. Contamination 
inflates apparent accuracy without any corresponding gain in reasoning ability. 
• Yes (2): contamination is either prevented or quantified. Prevented: items were created 
after the model's training cutoff, or are demonstrably inaccessible to the web. Quantified: 
The study reports whether evaluation items predate the model's training cutoff and 
describes how training-data contamination was minimized. 
• Partial (1): contamination is explicitly acknowledged as a limitation, but no mitigation or 
sensitivity analysis is undertaken. 
• No / NR (0): publicly available items are used with no mention of contamination. 
• NA: the task supplies all necessary information in -context and evaluates reasoning over 
the provided material rather than recall from training data (e.g., summarizing a provided 
operative note or simplifying a supplied patient information leaflet). 
 
S3. Is the sample size justified, with the unit of analysis specified? 
The unit of analysis is frequently ambiguous (powered on items, responses, raters, or runs ) and 
that ambiguity propagates into the statistical analysis (see 4.4). 
• Yes (2): an a priori power or precision calculation is reported, with the unit of analysis 
explicitly named. 
• Partial (1): the unit of analysis is clearly identifiable and the sample size is justified by 
reference to feasibility, precedent, or expected precision, without a formal calculation. 

=== PDF PAGE 37 ===
• No / NR (0): no justification offered, or the unit of analysis cannot be determined (e.g. "30 
questions were assessed by four raters across three runs" with no statement of what n refers 
to in the analysis). 
 
S4. Does the item set represent the spectrum of cases the system would encounter in the 
intended deployment setting? 
Published case reports, teaching collections, and question banks are each enriched for 
diagnostically informative, unusual, or pedagogically clean material, and so do not reflect the 
prevalence, complexity, or case mix of routine practice. The requirement is not that items be 
unselected, but that selection be described and its relationship to the deployment population 
addressed. 
• Yes (2) : items are drawn consecutively or unselectively from the target setting, or the 
distribution of diseases, case severity, or urgency in the evaluation set is shown to be 
representative of the intended deployment population. 
• Partial (1) : the item source is clearly described, and this is acknowledged as a limitation 
on generalizability. 
• No / NR (0)  : an enriched or curated source is used and results are presented as 
representative of clinical performance without qualification; or the source is insufficiently 
described to judge. 

=== PDF PAGE 38 ===
S5. Is the language of evaluation reported and appropriate for the intended deployment 
population? 
Performance varies substantially across languages and results in one language should not be 
assumed to transfer. Where intended users or patients are not monolingual in the evaluation 
language, single-language evaluation constrains what the study can claim. 
• Yes (2): the evaluation language is stated and either performance is assessed across the 
languages relevant to the intended setting, or single -language evaluation is explicitly 
justified by reference to that setting. 
• Partial (1) : the language is stated, evaluation is single -language, and the limitation is 
acknowledged without justification tied to deployment context. 
• No / NR (0): evaluation is single-language with conclusions generalized beyond it without 
qualification; or the evaluation language is not stated at all. 
4.2 I: Independence and blinding 
I1. Were raters blinded to response source, with blinding integrity verified? 
LLM prose has recognizable stylistic markers (characteristic length, enumerated structure, 
hedging) that allow source identification without clinical judgement. Blinding therefore requires 
active mitigation, not merely withholding labels. 
• Yes (2): raters were blinded and mitigation is described (normalizing formatting and length 
across arms, stripping enumeration and headers), and blinding success is verified, ideally 
by asking raters to guess the source and reporting their accuracy. 

=== PDF PAGE 39 ===
• Partial (1) : raters were blinded to source labels, but no formatting normalization or 
verification is reported. 
• No / NR (0): raters were unblinded, or blinding is not mentioned in a study whose design 
requires it. 
• NA: no human rating: responses scored automatically against a fixed answer key, or 
against automated metrics only. 
I2. Were two or more independent raters used, with agreement quantified? 
• Yes (2) 
• Partial (1): multiple raters used, with agreement reported as a bare point estimate or raw 
percentage agreement only, without CI. 
• No / NR (0): single rater, or multiple raters with no agreement statistic reported. 
• NA: no subjective rating involved (automated scoring against an objective key). 
I3. Is rater assignment free of confounding with the comparison of interest? 
In nested designs where each rater assesses only one model, rater leniency is perfectly confounded 
with model identity, and any observed between-model difference is uninterpretable. 
• Yes (2): all raters assessed all arms (fully crossed design), or a nested design includes an 
anchoring mechanism (a common item set rated by every rater) permitting estimation and 
adjustment of rater-specific leniency. 
• Partial (1) : a nested or partially crossed design is used, the confounding is explicitly 
acknowledged, but no anchoring or statistical adjustment is applied. 

=== PDF PAGE 40 ===
• No / NR (0) : a nested design is used with between -arm comparisons reported as though 
raters were interchangeable, with no acknowledgement; or the assignment structure cannot 
be determined. 
• NA: no human raters, or only one arm evaluated with no between-arm comparison. 
4.3 G: Ground truth and comparator fairness 
G1. Is the reference standard defensible and its derivation reported? 
• Yes (2): the reference standard's derivation is fully specified: for expert-panel ground truth, 
the number and subspecialty match of experts, their agreement, and the adjudication 
procedure; for record -derived truth, the criteria used and acknowledgement that the 
recorded diagnosis may itself be wrong; for answer keys, the source and its editorial 
provenance. 
• Partial (1) : the reference standard's source is stated but its derivation is incompletely 
specified (e.g. "confirmed by two retina specialists" with no agreement statistic or 
adjudication rule). 
• No / NR (0) : the reference standard is asserted without derivation. 
 
G2. Is the human comparator concurrent and tested on the identical item set? 
Comparison against historical benchmarks is not a controlled comparison: those figures reflect 
different examinee populations, testing conditions, time pressure, and stakes. 

=== PDF PAGE 41 ===
• Yes (2) : human comparators were tested concurrently on the identical item set, under stated 
conditions. 
• Partial (1): a human comparator is used but drawn from a non-concurrent or non-identical 
source (historical pass rates, vendor norms, previously published clinician answers), and 
this limitation is explicitly acknowledged. 
• No / NR (0) : human-comparative claims ("comparable to ophthalmologists," "exceeded 
the human pass rate") are made on the basis of non -concurrent benchmarks without 
qualification. 
• NA: the study makes no human-comparative claim, evaluating model performance against 
a reference standard only. 
Applicability: All domains, but note the NA condition carefully; a study that never claims human 
comparability should not be penalized for lacking a comparator. The distinction is between not 
comparing (NA) and comparing badly (No). 
G3. Did model and human arms receive equivalent clinical information? 
• Yes (2) : both arms received identical inputs, or asymmetries are documented and either 
equalized or accounted for analytically. 

=== PDF PAGE 42 ===
• Partial (1): an information asymmetry exists (the clinician had the chart, examination, or 
prior relationship; the model had a text string) and is explicitly acknowledged as limiting 
interpretation. 
• No / NR (0) : an asymmetry exists and is not acknowledged, so the study measures the 
information difference while attributing it to reasoning capability. 
• NA: no human comparator arm. 
4.4 H: Handling of paired and repeated-measures data 
Most comparative LLM studies evaluate multiple models on the same set of questions or clinical 
cases. Because each model is tested on identical items, the observations are paired rather than 
independent, and the analysis should reflect this design. Treatin g paired observations as 
independent ignores the correlation between model responses and can lead to incorrect estimates 
of variability, misleading p -values, inappropriate confidence intervals, and loss of statistical 
power. It may also exaggerate or obscure differences between models, making conclusions about 
comparative performance unreliable. Studies should therefore use statistical methods appropriate 
for paired or repeated -measures data, or explicitly justify why an independent analysis is 
appropriate. 
H1. Is the statistical analysis matched to the paired or repeated-measures structure? 
When multiple models answer the same items, the resulting accuracy vectors are repeated 
measures on shared items, not independent samples. Item difficulty is a shared unmeasured 
influence on all arms. 

=== PDF PAGE 43 ===
• Yes (2): analysis matches the data structure. 
• Partial (1): a paired test is used where a mixed model would better separate item and rater 
effects, or the design is treated as paired in some analyses and independent in others without 
explanation. 
• No / NR (0)  : independent-samples tests applied to paired data. Also scored 0 where the 
analysis performed cannot be determined. 
• NA: a single model evaluated with no between-arm or between-condition comparison, and 
no repeated measurement. 
A further point on clustering.  Items drawn from the same subspecialty, the same question 
bank, the same source website  or multiple runs from the same model are not independent of 
one another. Where the item set has such structure, it should be modelled as a random effect 
or via cluster-robust standard errors rather than ignored. 
 
H2. Is multiplicity controlled? 
• Yes (2): a primary outcome is pre -specified and multiplicity is formally controlled, with 
the number of comparisons stated. 
• Partial (1): correction is applied to some comparison families and not others. 

=== PDF PAGE 44 ===
• No / NR (0) : multiple pairwise comparisons across models and outcome dimensions are 
reported uncorrected. 
• NA: a single pre-specified comparison only. 
H3. Are effect sizes and confidence intervals reported alongside p-values? 
• Yes (2): effect estimates with confidence intervals for all primary comparisons. 
• Partial (1) : effect sizes reported without confidence intervals, or CIs given for some 
comparisons only. 
• No / NR (0): p-values reported alone. Given typical sample sizes in this literature, a bare 
significance statement conveys almost nothing about clinical magnitude. 
H4. Was potential demographic bias in model outputs evaluated? 
The concern is whether the model's responses change because of patient characteristics (such as 
age, sex, or ethnicity) rather than because of clinically relevant differences. For example, two 
patients with the same presentation should not receive differen t urgency recommendations or 
management advice simply because they differ in age, sex, or ethnicity, unless there is a clear 
clinical reason. 
• Yes (2): The study evaluates whether model performance or responses differ according to 
at least one patient demographic characteristic. This may include comparing performance 
across demographic groups or using identical clinical vignettes that differ only in age, sex, 
or ethnicity.  
• Partial (1): Patient demographic characteristics are reported but their relationship with 
model performance is not analyzed.  

=== PDF PAGE 45 ===
• No / NR (0): Patient demographic information is included in the evaluation cases or 
prompts but is neither reported nor analyzed.  
• NA: The evaluation items contain no patient demographic information.  
Applicability by domain: 
• Examinations: Usually NA, as examination questions typically do not include patient 
demographic information.  
• Patient education: Assess whether the quality, readability, or recommendations vary 
according to patient demographics.  
• Diagnosis: Assess whether changing only demographic characteristics (while keeping the 
clinical presentation identical) changes the model's diagnosis.  
• Triage: Assess whether urgency or referral recommendations differ according to patient 
demographics despite identical clinical presentations.  
• Documentation: Assess whether the tone, level of detail, or completeness of generated 
documentation differs according to patient demographics. 
4.5 T: Transparency and trustworthiness 
T1. Is the exact model version, access date, and interface reported? 
Providers ship revisions under unchanged public names; several distinct snapshots of a single 
model may be released within one year. 

=== PDF PAGE 46 ===
• Yes (2): the precise version string or snapshot identifier, the access or query date, and the 
interface used (web interface versus application programming interface [API])  are all 
reported. 
• Partial (1): major version and approximate date given ("GPT -4, accessed March 2024") 
without snapshot identifier or interface. 
• No / NR (0): only a model family named ("ChatGPT," "Gemini") with no version or date. 
T2. Is the stability of the performance estimate established? 
LLM output is stochastic. A study querying each item once has drawn a single observation from a 
distribution and reported it as a point estimate. Setting temperature to zero is a partial mitigation 
only, so determinism should be demonstrated rather than assumed. 
• Yes (2): each item queried multiple times with both central tendency and dispersion across 
runs reported; generation parameters (temperature, top-p, seed) stated where accessible, or 
their inaccessibility declared for web-interface studies. 
• Partial (1) : multiple runs performed but only aggregate results reported without 
dispersion; or a single query per item with generation parameters fully disclosed and 
stochasticity explicitly acknowledged as a limitation. 
• No / NR (0): single query per item with no parameter reporting and no acknowledgement 
of stochasticity; or determinism asserted on the basis of temperature = 0 alone. 
 
T3. Are prompts disclosed verbatim, with prompt sensitivity considered? 

=== PDF PAGE 47 ===
• Yes (2) : full prompts reproduced verbatim in text or supplement, and sensitivity to 
plausible prompt variants assessed or explicitly addressed. 
• Partial (1): prompts disclosed verbatim with no sensitivity analysis. 
• No / NR (0) : prompts paraphrased, summarized, or omitted, so the study cannot be 
replicated. 
T4. Where the study claims clinical usefulness, is model confidence evaluated? 
Accuracy shows how often a model is correct. Calibration shows whether the model is more 
confident when it is correct and less confident when it is wrong. This item applies only if the study 
makes claims about clinical use, clinical readiness, or suitability for diagnosis, triage, or screening. 
Studies that report benchmark performance without making these claims are not expected to assess 
calibration.  For example, if an LLM labels 100 fundus photographs as diabetic retinopathy with 
90% confidence, a well-calibrated model should be correct in about 90 of those cases. If only 60 
are truly diabetic retinopathy, the model is overconfident despite appearing highly certain. 
• Yes (2): The study evaluates the relationship between model confidence and correctness. 
For triage or screening applications, it also reports how the model handles uncertain cases, 
such as abstaining from answering or recommending clinician review.  
• Partial (1): The study reports model confidence but does not compare it with correctness. 
Alternatively, uncertainty or abstention is described but not quantified, consistency across 
repeated runs is used instead of a direct calibration assessment, or the authors acknowledge 
that the lack of calibration limits their conclusions about clinical use.  

=== PDF PAGE 48 ===
• No / NR (0): The study claims the model is suitable for clinical use, or ready for 
deployment without evaluating model confidence or acknowledging this limitation.  
• NA: The study limits its conclusions to the measured task (e.g., benchmark performance) 
and makes no claims about clinical use, safety, or deployment. 
T5. Where the study draws conclusions from the model's explanations, are those 
explanations evaluated? 
A correct answer does not necessarily mean the model used correct reasoning. Likewise, a 
convincing explanation or attention map does not prove that the model actually based its decision 
on those reasons. Studies that claim a model is interpretable, transparent, or clinically trustworthy 
because of its explanations should evaluate those explanations directly, rather than assume they 
are reliable. 
This item applies only when the study uses the model's explanations to support its conclusions. 
Studies that evaluate only the final answer, or provide example explanations without drawing 
conclusions from them, are not expected to meet this criterion. 
• Yes (2): The study evaluates the quality or validity of the model's explanations 
independently of whether the final answer is correct. Examples include expert assessment 
of the reasoning, testing whether changing the explanation changes the prediction, or 
comparing highlighted image regions with expert-annotated diagnostic features.  
• Partial (1): The study rates explanations as plausible or useful but does not evaluate 
whether they reflect the model's actual reasoning. Alternatively, the authors acknowledge 
that the lack of such evaluation limits their conclusions.  

=== PDF PAGE 49 ===
• No / NR (0): The study claims the model is interpretable, transparent, or clinically 
trustworthy based on its explanations or attention maps, but provides no evaluation of 
whether those explanations are valid.  
• NA: The study evaluates only the model's final outputs and does not base any conclusions 
on its explanations.

5. Limitations 
This is a narrative review and is therefore susceptible to selection bias in the studies discussed. We 
did not perform duplicate screening, did not register a protocol, and did not attempt exhaustive 
capture; readers requiring a complete enumeration should consult the existing systematic reviews. 
The SIGHT framework should be regarded as a preliminary methodological framework rather than 
a validated risk-of-bias instrument. It was derived inductively from observed failure modes in the 
reviewed corpus, not through formal Delphi consensus, and its items have not been tested for inter-
rater reliability(Diamond et al., 2014; Moher et al., 2010). We present it as a structured argument 
and a practical checklist, not as a validated instrument, and we would welcome formal 
development. 

=== PDF PAGE 53 ===
Finally, this review shares the currency problem it criticizes. Our search cutoff is stated, and model 
capabilities will have changed by publication. We have tried to mitigate this by emphasizing design 
principles, which are stable, over performance figures, which are not. 
 
6. Conclusions 
Large language models have reached a level of performance that makes questions of evaluation 
more important than questions of capability. Across ophthalmology, benchmark accuracy has 
improved rapidly, but evaluation methodology has not kept pace. Common weaknesses (including 
reliance on curated datasets, inadequate handling of paired study designs, limited assessment of 
uncertainty and calibration, insufficient attention to comparator fairness, and frequent 
overinterpretation of benchmark results) limit confidence in claims of clinical readiness. 
The SIGHT framework was developed to address these recurring methodological shortcomings. 
Rather than introducing another reporting checklist, it provides a structured appraisal framework 
focused on whether the conclusions of an LLM evaluation are justified by its design, analysis, and 
interpretation. By emphasizing methodological validity alongside reporting quality, SIGHT 
complements existing guidance such as TRIPOD-LLM (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable 
prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis –Large Language Models), CHART 
(Chatbot Assessment Reporting Tool), and AQAT:RoB. 
We do not present SIGHT as a validated risk -of-bias instrument. Instead, it is a framework based 
on recurring methodological limitations identified across the current ophthalmic LLM literature. 
Future work should refine its content through expert consensus, evaluate its inter-rater reliability, 
and examine its performance across broade r medical specialties. As LLMs continue to evolve, 

=== PDF PAGE 54 ===
improving how they are evaluated may be more important than incremental improvements in 
model accuracy. A trustworthy evaluation framework is essential if these systems are to be 
translated safely from benchmark studies into routine ophthalmic care.
