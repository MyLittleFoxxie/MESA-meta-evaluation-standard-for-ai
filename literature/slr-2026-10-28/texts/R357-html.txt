Go to: Abstract Purpose Artificial intelligence is reshaping medical imaging, yet reliable bias assessment in this context remains insufficiently studied. It remains unclear whether the widely used QUADAS-2 checklist provides reproducible evaluations in AI-based imaging studies. Therefore, we compared human and ChatGPT-4o assessments to examine the reproducibility of QUADAS-2 in this setting. Materials and Methods Twenty PubMed-indexed AI-based dental imaging studies were analyzed by five human reviewers and five independent ChatGPT-4o runs using identical categorical outputs. Reliability within and between groups was quantified using Fleiss’ kappa. Results Human agreement ranged from −0.07 to 0.22, with only the reference standard domain reaching fair reliability (κ=0.22, P <0.001). ChatGPT-4o produced kappa values from −0.17 to 0.56, with the highest consistency observed in the index test domain (κ=0.56, P <0.001). Between-group kappa values remained near zero across domains, except in items uniformly classified as low risk. These patterns suggest that QUADAS-2 may yield inconsistent judgments across both groups, despite evaluation of 20 studies and repeated AI classifications. Conclusion These findings suggest that QUADAS-2 may have limited reproducibility when applied to AI-based imaging research under the present study conditions, although the observed inconsistency may also reflect features of the study design and assessment setting. These findings support consideration of AI-specific tools such as QUADAS-AI for future bias assessment in AI-driven medical imaging. Keywords Artificial Intelligence ; Radiography ; Dental ; Image Interpretation ; Computer-Assisted ; Reproducibility of Results ; Observer Variation Go to: Introduction Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning are transforming medical imaging, surpassing traditional statistical approaches to improve diagnostic accuracy, efficiency, and workflow consistency. 1 , 2 , 3 Methods such as support vector machines, ensemble trees, and deep neural networks have shown strong performance across diverse clinical applications. 4 , 5 However, safe clinical adoption requires rigorous evaluation of reliability and trustworthiness, particularly regarding bias, which can undermine validity, limit generalizability, and pose risks to patient safety. 6 , 7 , 8 Risk of bias in this context refers to systematic errors in model development or evaluation that distort performance estimates in the target population. 9 , 10 Such errors, arising from data provenance, algorithmic design, human decisions, or broader ethical/economic factors, may lead to inflated or underestimated accuracy and inappropriate clinical decisions. 11 , 12 Structured evaluation frameworks are essential for addressing these issues. The Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies(QUADAS-2) checklist is widely used for assessing diagnostic studies, rating risk of bias as “low,” “high,” or “unclear” across four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow/timing. 13 Complementary guidelines, such as TRIPOD-AI for transparent reporting of prediction models(including AI/machine learning methods) and FUTURE-AI principles, further promote reproducibility and methodological rigor in AI research. 14 , 15 Despite widespread application, QUADAS-2 was designed for conventional diagnostic accuracy studies and assumes binary outputs, whereas AI models frequently yield probabilistic or multi-class predictions. 16 This mismatch may overlook AI-specific biases, including data leakage, inadequate external validation, class imbalance, and algorithmic bias. 7 , 17 , 18 These limitations have prompted the development of QUADAS-AI, an extension incorporating additional signaling questions and new domains for data quality, model performance/analysis, and explainability/transparency. 19 , 20 Despite widespread use, the reproducibility of QUADAS-2 in AI-based imaging has not been empirically tested. It remains unclear whether QUADAS-2 is sufficient on its own, requires AI-specific supplementation, or should be supplanted by newer frameworks for robust appraisal. 17 , 18 , 21 , 22 This study therefore evaluates the reliability of the QUADAS-2 checklist for bias assessment in AI-based medical imaging research. By comparing assessments from human reviewers and repeated ChatGPT-4o classifications, it aims to determine its suitability for this rapidly evolving field. Go to: Materials and Methods Study design This was a methodological evaluation study of inter-rater reliability, designed to compare the performance of human reviewers and ChatGPT-4o in applying the QUADAS-2 checklist to AI-based medical imaging research. A total of 20 PubMed-indexed AI-based dental imaging studies were selected to evaluate the reproducibility of the QUADAS-2 checklist. The sample size was selected pragmatically to balance feasibility of repeated human and AI assessment with the need to include variation across QUADAS-2 items. The primary aim of the study was not statistical generalization to all AI imaging literature, but evaluation of the reproducibility of checklist-based assessments within this sample. Risk of bias was assessed independently by five human reviewers with prior experience using QUADAS-2, and in parallel by ChatGPT-4o across five repeated runs. All assessments were restricted to the three standard QUADAS-2 categories (“low risk,” “high risk,” and “unclear risk”). Agreement was quantified using Fleiss’ kappa (κ) to compare ratings within the human group, within the AI group, and between groups. Study selection For this methodological reliability study, we included 20 PubMed-indexed articles on AI-based dental imaging. The studies were identified through a pragmatic PubMed search using combinations of terms related to artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, dental imaging, panoramic radiography, cone-beam computed tomography, and diagnostic imaging. The search was intended to identify a feasible set of recent and relevant AI-based dental imaging studies rather than to establish a systematic review dataset. Studies were considered eligible if they were English-language, PubMed-indexed articles that applied an artificial intelligence or machine learning method to dental or maxillofacial imaging data. Eligible studies included diagnostic, detection, segmentation, classification, or prediction tasks based on dental imaging modalities. Dental imaging studies were specifically selected because all human reviewers had a dental academic background, which reduced variability related to differences in disciplinary expertise. The final number of 20 studies was selected pragmatically to balance feasibility with sufficient variation across QUADAS-2 domains. Because each study was assessed by five human reviewers and five independent ChatGPT-4o runs across seven domain-level judgments, this sample generated a substantial number of categorical assessments for reliability analysis while remaining manageable for repeated independent evaluation. Accordingly, the study sample was assembled as a pragmatic methodological set rather than through a formal systematic-review search and screening workflow. The purpose of article selection was not to generate a representative sample of all AI-based imaging studies, but to provide a feasible and relevant group of AI-based dental imaging studies for repeated human and AI classification using the QUADAS-2 framework. This sampling approach should be interpreted as a study limitation, as it may reduce reproducibility, limit generalizability, and introduce selection bias. QUADAS-2 was applied at the domain level because the primary objective of this study was to compare the reproducibility of the final checklist judgments produced by human reviewers and repeated ChatGPT-4o assessments under a common and directly comparable output structure. Specifically, four risk-of-bias domains were assessed: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. In addition, applicability concerns were assessed for the first three domains: patient selection, index test, and reference standard. Thus, each study received seven QUADAS-2 domain-level judgments in total. Within each domain, QUADAS-2 signaling questions were used in the standard way to inform one overall domain judgment of “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear risk,” rather than being analyzed as separate outcomes. In other words, the signaling questions served as decision aids for arriving at the final domain-level classification, consistent with the intended structure of QUADAS-2. 13 This approach was selected because the study focused on agreement in the final judgments typically reported in evidence appraisal, and because domain-level outputs allowed direct comparison between human reviewers and ChatGPT-4o using the same categorical format. However, this approach may have reduced some of the granularity of the original signaling-question structure. Because these seven judgments included both risk-of-bias and applicability assessments, they were analyzed as a shared set of domain-level outputs for reproducibility purposes and not as a single psychometric construct. Human reviewer assessment Five independent reviewers were recruited. All reviewers had a dental academic background and prior experience in methodological appraisal; however, their individual levels of familiarity with AI-based diagnostic imaging studies were not formally measured, which should be considered when interpreting inter-rater variability. To intentionally simulate a real-world assessment scenario, no calibration or group training session was conducted before the study. This design choice was made a priori to evaluate how reproducibly the checklist performs when applied independently under routine conditions, rather than after consensus-building or calibration. Instead, each reviewer was provided with the official QUADAS-2 guideline and accompanying explanatory article and asked to independently assess the 20 selected articles. Reviewers used the QUADAS-2 signaling questions within each domain to inform one final domain-level judgment of “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear risk,” in accordance with the standard QUADAS-2 approach. AI assessment For the AI-based assessments, we used ChatGPT-4o. The ChatGPT-4o prompt instructed the model to act as an AI researcher specializing in healthcare and to assess each included study using the QUADAS-2 framework. The model was provided with the QUADAS-2 domains, signaling questions, and definitions for risk of bias and applicability concerns. For each article, ChatGPT-4o was instructed to assign one of three judgments, “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear risk,” for the four risk-of-bias domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. It was also instructed to assign “low,” “high,” or “unclear” concern for the three applicability domains: patient selection, index test, and reference standard. The model was instructed to base its judgments on the provided study text and QUADAS-2 guidance, to use “unclear” when information was missing or ambiguous, and to provide concise justifications for each judgment. In brief, the model was supplied with the official QUADAS-2 guideline in PDF format verbatim, without paraphrasing, followed by the full text of each included study pasted into the chat for evaluation. The model was instructed to use the signaling questions within each QUADAS-2 domain to arrive at one final domain-level judgment, rather than to output separate judgments for individual signaling questions. To ensure outputs were directly comparable with human ratings, responses were restricted to the three standard QUADAS-2 categories: “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear risk.” This restriction was explicitly enforced in the prompt. After completing the evaluation of all 20 articles, the chat session was deleted to reduce memory carryover, and the process was repeated five times using the same prompt structure to assess consistency. As a result, all assessments were categorical and required no post-processing or re-classification before statistical analysis. ChatGPT-4o was accessed through the web version of ChatGPT in August 2025. Because the specific dates of the individual assessment sessions within August 2025 were not recorded, this should be considered a limitation for strict reproducibility. Reliability analysis We calculated Fleiss’ κ to assess inter-rater reliability across three conditions: 1) reviewer - reviewer: agreement among the five human assessors, 2) AI - AI: agreement among ChatGPT-4o outputs across the five independent runs, and 3) reviewer - AI: agreement between pooled human assessments and pooled AI outputs. The unit of analysis was the study-level domain judgment. For each of the 20 included studies, each rater assigned one categorical rating to each of the seven domain-level judgments, and these ratings were used in the agreement analysis. Fleiss’ κ was chosen because it is specifically designed for measuring agreement among multiple raters when assigning categorical ratings. Values of κ were interpreted according to Landis and Koch’s benchmarks: <0.00 (poor), 0.00–0.20 (slight), 0.21–0.40 (fair), 0.41–0.60 (moderate), 0.61–0.80 (substantial), and 0.81–1.00 (almost perfect agreement). We selected Fleiss’ κ because the assessments were categorical on three nominal levels(“low risk,” “high risk,” “unclear risk”), without ordinal or interval properties. In such cases, alternatives such as weighted Fleiss’ κ (appropriate for ordinal data) or the intraclass correlation coefficient(appropriate for continuous or interval-scaled data) are not suitable. Additionally, because our study involved more than two raters(five human reviewers and five AI runs), Cohen’s kappa was not applicable; Fleiss’ κ is specifically designed for multiple raters on nominal data. Each assessment was required to fall into exactly one of the three categories, and no partial agreement was defined. Thus, complete agreement was only recorded when all raters selected the same category, while any divergence was treated as disagreement. Because several items showed marked category imbalance, Fleiss’ κ values were interpreted cautiously. In such settings, κ may appear low or even negative despite concentration of ratings within a single category. Therefore, the agreement results in this study should be interpreted together with the observed category distributions presented in the Tables, rather than on the basis of κ values alone. Go to: Discussion This study evaluated the reliability of the QUADAS-2 checklist for assessing risk of bias in AI-based medical imaging research, comparing the performance of human reviewers and ChatGPT-4o. Our results showed that both groups achieved only slight to fair levels of agreement, with the highest consistency observed in the index test domain for the AI(Q2, κ=0.56) and the reference standard domain for humans(Q3, κ=0.22). Between-group agreement was negligible, except in items with highly uniform category distributions. Under the conditions of this study, these findings suggest that QUADAS-2 may show limited reproducibility when applied to AI-based imaging research. The low Fleiss’ κ among human reviewers aligns with prior evidence that methodological appraisal tools can suffer from subjective interpretation, even when reviewers are trained and experienced. 11 , 13 Kelly et al. 6 and Char et al. 8 have emphasized that achieving reliable evaluation in AI is particularly challenging because sources of bias are multifactorial, spanning algorithmic design, data provenance, and human decision-making. Our findings reinforce this concern: even with standardized criteria, interpretations varied substantially, echoing Varoquaux and Cheplygina’s 9 observation that methodological weaknesses in AI imaging research often go undetected due to inconsistent assessment practices. ChatGPT-4o showed stronger internal consistency in the index test domain, where the operational criteria were relatively straightforward. However, agreement dropped in other items, including patient selection and flow/timing. Taken together with the low agreement among human reviewers, this pattern suggests that reproducibility was constrained not only by the assessor, but also by the structure and fit of the instrument being applied. In this context, both human reviewers and ChatGPT-4o appear to have been limited by the ambiguity of applying QUADAS-2 to AI-based imaging studies. 10 , 21 , 22 Another important observation is that the limited reproducibility of ChatGPT-4o classifications in this study should not be interpreted solely as a model limitation. The same checklist also produced low agreement among human reviewers, suggesting that the observed inconsistency may reflect not only assessor-related factors but also the difficulty of applying QUADAS-2 to AI-based imaging studies in this study setting. Because the tool was originally designed for diagnostic accuracy studies, 13 some of its signaling questions may not fully align with methodological issues that are especially relevant in AI research, such as data leakage, class imbalance, or limited external validation. 7 , 17 , 18 However, our findings do not by themselves establish that QUADAS-2 is inherently unsuitable in all AI imaging contexts. Rather, they suggest that AI-specific appraisal frameworks, such as QUADAS-AI, 19 , 20 may provide a better methodological fit and should be evaluated in future studies. More cautiously, the poor reproducibility observed here may indicate limitations in the fit of QUADAS-2 for this context, although the observed pattern may also have been influenced by the pragmatic article sample, the absence of reviewer calibration, and the repeated prompt-based LLM assessment design. The tool was originally developed for conventional diagnostic accuracy studies, 13 and it may not fully capture some AI-relevant methodological issues such as probabilistic or multi-class outputs, 16 data leakage, class imbalance, or the absence of external validation. 7 , 17 , 18 These concerns have motivated the development of QUADAS-AI, which introduces additional domains related to data quality, model performance, and transparency. 19 , 20 Our findings therefore support further evaluation and use of AI-specific appraisal frameworks in future methodological studies. This study has several limitations. It was restricted to 20 dental imaging papers selected as a pragmatic methodological sample, which limits generalizability to broader medical imaging contexts and introduces the possibility of selection bias. Only one LLM, ChatGPT-4o, was evaluated, and future studies should compare multiple models to examine the robustness of AI-assisted bias assessment. QUADAS-2 was applied at the domain level, yielding seven domain-level judgments per study across four risk-of-bias domains and three applicability domains. Although this enabled standardized comparison across human reviewers and AI runs, it may have reduced some of the granularity of the original signaling-question structure. In addition, these seven judgments should not be interpreted as a single unified construct, because they included both risk-of-bias and applicability assessments. The observed agreement patterns may therefore reflect not only characteristics of QUADAS-2, but also features of the study design, including the pragmatic article sample, the absence of reviewer calibration, and the prompt-based repeated LLM classification workflow. In addition, although the AI assessment procedure was standardized across runs, the specific dates of the web-based ChatGPT-4o sessions conducted in August 2025 were not recorded, which limits strict technical reproducibility. Finally, Fleiss’ κ was the only agreement statistic applied. Although appropriate for multiple raters assigning nominal categories, κ is sensitive to category prevalence and may underestimate agreement when category distributions are highly imbalanced. Additional agreement measures and confidence intervals would have provided a more comprehensive assessment and should be considered in future studies. Despite these limitations, this is, to our knowledge, the first study to directly compare human and AI-assisted application of QUADAS-2 in medical imaging research. Our findings underscore three points: 1) reproducibility of QUADAS-2 is limited when applied to AI studies; 2) LLMs show potential as standardization aids but remain insufficient as independent assessors; and 3) there is a clear need to adopt or refine AI-specific appraisal frameworks such as QUADAS-AI. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Future work should also consider applying alternative reporting or bias assessment frameworks such as CLAIM (2024), 43 TRIPOD-AI, 14 and FUTURE-AI, 44 which may complement QUADAS-AI in addressing the broader methodological and ethical challenges of AI in medical imaging.
